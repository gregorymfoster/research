## Overview

The companion post argued that a good second mountain is chosen deliberately, by importance and personal fit. This post is the survey that argument implies: what the big problems actually are in 2026, and what principles, drawn from history, separate a good big problem from a bad one.

The question is asked from a specific position: an individual with capital, engineering skill, and time. Not a government, not a foundation with a hundred program officers. That position changes the answer. Some problems below are bottlenecked on money, and a single funder can matter. Some are bottlenecked on talent, where one builder is the difference. Some are bottlenecked on knowledge or institutions, and what they need is someone willing to run a long experiment.

The method was to go to primary sources: the original talk, the Nobel lecture, the official report, the paper itself. Where a famous number could not be traced to a source that owns it, I say so. The principles come first, because they are the lens. The survey follows. Twelve problems made the cut, and I have tried to be honest about which are crowded, which are stuck, and what kind of contribution each one actually absorbs.

## Part one: what makes a big problem good

### Hamming: importance means you have an attack

The single most useful text on choosing problems is still Richard Hamming's 1986 Bell Communications Research talk, [You and Your Research](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html) (first-person testimony from a scientist who spent decades watching who did great work and who did not). The famous line is blunt: "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work."

But Hamming immediately guards the word "important" against the obvious misreading. Bell Labs did not work on time travel, teleportation, or antigravity, even though any of them was worth a Nobel Prize and any sum of money: "They are not important problems because we do not have an attack. It's not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack."

Three other observations from the talk survive contact with everything else I read. First, great scientists hold ten to twenty important problems in mind and pounce when a new tool makes one of them attackable. Second, courage is a prerequisite, and Hamming treats it as trainable: "Once you get your courage up and believe that you can do important problems, then you can." Third, the open door: in Hamming's words, he who works with the door open "gets all kinds of interruptions, but he also occasionally gets clues as to what the world is and what might be important," and over ten years the open-door people did the work that mattered. For someone leaving an operating role, the open door is a warning against building a private research bunker.

![Two boxes: consequence without an attack holds time travel, teleportation, and antigravity; consequence with an attack holds a new tool, a new strategy, or a new institution](https://research.gmfoster.com/assets/illustrations/bigger-problems/01-framework-hamming-attack.jpg)

### Hilbert: pick a field that is generating problems

David Hilbert's [1900 address to the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/hilbert/problems.html) is the archetype of the problem list, and its preamble is a theory of problem choice (traditional wisdom from the mathematician who wrote the most consequential problem list ever). The test of a field: "As long as a branch of science offers an abundance of problems, so long is it alive," while "a lack of problems foreshadows extinction or the cessation of independent development."

Hilbert also gives criteria for a single good problem: it should be clear enough to explain "to the first man whom you meet on the street," and it should be "difficult in order to entice us, yet not completely inaccessible, lest it mock at our efforts." Difficulty calibrated just past the edge of current tools. And he supplies the emotional engine, the conviction that an answer exists: "We hear within us the perpetual call: There is the problem. Seek its solution."

The 2026 application is direct. Ask of each candidate problem: is this field generating new, well-posed subproblems this year, or is it recycling the same stuck ones. That single test separates most of the survey items below into live and stalled.

### The frameworks: scale, neglect, tractability, and their limits

The formalization of problem choice comes from the effective altruism world. [80,000 Hours' problem framework](https://80000hours.org/articles/problem-framework/) scores problems on scale, neglectedness, and solvability, plus personal fit, on logarithmic scales (this is an organization's own reasoning: primary for the framework, not neutral evidence, and the page itself lists the framework's weaknesses). The page credits the framework's creation to Coefficient Giving, the name Open Philanthropy now carries on its own pages.

The complementary document is [Hits-based giving](https://coefficientgiving.org/research/hits-based-giving/), Open Philanthropy's essay on why a philanthropic portfolio should accept many failures for a few enormous successes (again, the organization's own reasoning). Two of its arguments matter most for an individual. First, structural neglect: "If an idea is backed by strong evidence, expert consensus, and obvious appeal, it's probably already well-funded." Second, the anti-consensus point: "Consensus tends to filter out distinctive, unusual ideas." Their receipts include early funding of David Baker's machine-learning protein design lab (Baker shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, per the same page) and being the first major funder of the YIMBY housing movement.

The same page repeats the claim that the Green Revolution is "credited with saving roughly one billion people from starvation." I could not trace the billion-lives figure to a study that owns it, and I treat it as shaky: widely repeated, provenance murky. The verifiable version is in Borlaug's own Nobel lecture, below.

The frameworks are a triage tool; they do not settle anything. The 80,000 Hours page says as much about itself. Neglectedness in particular misfires for builders: a crowded field can still have empty niches, and an empty field can be empty because it has no attack (Hamming's point, restated in economic clothing).

### Existential risk: the stakes argument

Toby Ord's [The Precipice](https://theprecipice.com/) (2020) supplies the strongest version of the scale argument: humanity could last millions of generations, and risks from nuclear weapons, engineered pandemics, and unaligned AI put all of it at stake (the author's own framing, from the book's site). In the book Ord states his personal estimate of total existential risk this century at one in six. I could not fetch the page containing that figure, so treat my citation of the exact number as from the book itself rather than independently verified. Whatever one thinks of the number, the framing explains why AI safety, biosecurity, and nuclear risk all appear in this survey even where the attacks are thin: the stakes argument keeps them on the list.

### What the historical wins have in common

I looked for primary accounts of big problems that were chosen well and attacked well. A pattern shows up in every one: the problem ripened when a new tool or a new institution arrived, and the winners were the people positioned to notice.

The Longitude Prize is the cleanest institutional case. The [Longitude Act of 1714](https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1311) (the act's own text) offered tiered rewards, 10,000 to 20,000 pounds by accuracy, for a practical method of finding longitude at sea, and created a board empowered to fund promising work in advance. The prize did not solve the problem. It created the conditions for John Harrison, a carpenter's son with a new tool (precision timekeeping), to solve it, though the [Royal Museums Greenwich account](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem) records that the board paid him in stages over decades and he collected the balance only after appeals to Parliament and the King. Prizes generate solutions and then underpay them: a lesson for anyone designing incentives today.

Haber-Bosch is the tool case. Fritz Haber's [1918 Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1918/haber/lecture/) describes the synthesis of ammonia from its elements. A century later, [Erisman and colleagues in Nature Geoscience](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo325) put it plainly: "we live in a world transformed by and highly dependent upon Haber-Bosch nitrogen" (empirical, from the paper's own text). The widely quoted claim that synthetic nitrogen feeds roughly half of humanity traces to Vaclav Smil's work and to Erisman's paper, whose full text sits behind a paywall I could not open, so I flag the precise fraction as owned by those sources rather than verified here. The shape of the case is what matters: fixing nitrogen was an ancient problem that became attackable the moment high-pressure catalytic chemistry existed.

Smallpox is the strategy case. The programme's own book-length account is [Smallpox and its Eradication](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/39485) (Fenner, Henderson, and colleagues, WHO, 1988). D. A. Henderson, who directed the WHO campaign, spent his later years correcting the record in his own voice: in [a 2008 interview in the Bulletin of the WHO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2649583/) he calls the idea that eradication was a mass vaccination program "a myth" (first-person testimony). The campaign's engine was surveillance and containment: find cases, ring-vaccinate contacts. Henderson recounts that William Foege's team stopped outbreaks in eastern Nigeria having vaccinated only about 750,000 of 12 million people. The tool that mattered was not the vaccine, which was centuries old. It was a measurement and response strategy, plus an institution (an intensified WHO programme) that could execute it in every country at once.

Polio is the funding-institution case. The [March of Dimes' own history](https://www.marchofdimes.org/about-us/mission/history/history-march-dimes) (institutional first-person account) describes how a foundation built from millions of small donations funded Jonas Salk, ran the 1954 field trial with 1.8 million schoolchildren, and saw the vaccine licensed on April 12, 1955, after which polio "declined rapidly from tens of thousands of new cases per year to a mere handful." A private organization, not a government, financed the largest medical trial of its era and defined the endpoint that settled the question.

The Green Revolution is the person-plus-package case. Norman Borlaug's [1970 Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1970/borlaug/lecture/) (first-person testimony) insists "There are no miracles in agricultural production." Behind the yield takeoff were two decades of wheat breeding in Mexico, and the deployment package mattered as much as the seed: fertilizer, credit, and government price policy moved together. His own numbers: Pakistan's wheat crop rose from 4.6 million tons in 1965 to 8.4 million in 1970, reaching self-sufficiency in 1968, and India's from 12.3 to 20 million tons over the same period.

Operation Warp Speed is the modern compression case. The program's leaders, Moncef Slaoui and Matthew Hepburn, published [their own strategy account in the New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMp2027405) in August 2020 (first-person institutional testimony): a portfolio of eight vaccine candidates across four platform technologies, with clinical development, process development, and manufacturing scale-up run "fully resourced, in parallel," accepting the financial risk of building factories for vaccines that might fail. That is hits-based giving executed by a state at wartime speed.

Finally, the charter for all of it: Vannevar Bush's 1945 report [Science, the Endless Frontier](https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/2023-04/EndlessFrontier75th_w.pdf) argued that "basic research is the pacemaker of technological progress" and that "without scientific progress no amount of achievement in other directions can insure our health, prosperity, and security as a nation in the modern world." Bush's answer to the problem of sustaining discovery was, characteristically, a new institution: the report is the founding document of the National Science Foundation.

### The principles, stated

Each of these is tied to the sources above.

1. Importance means you have an attack. Consequence alone does not make a problem important (Hamming). Before committing, name the tool or strategy that makes the problem newly attackable.
2. Problems ripen when tools arrive. Longitude ripened with precision clockmaking, nitrogen with high-pressure chemistry, eradication with surveillance and containment, vaccines at speed with platform technologies and parallel finance (the cases above). The corollary for 2026: re-rank every old problem against the new enablers, cheap sequencing, cheap launch, new energy tech, and above all AI.
3. Pick fields that generate problems. A live field throws off new, well-posed questions each year (Hilbert). A stalled one recycles its stuck ones.
4. Use scale, neglect, and tractability for triage only. The framework's own authors list its weaknesses (80,000 Hours). Neglect can mean opportunity or it can mean no attack exists.
5. Run a portfolio and distrust consensus. The historical hits were non-consensus bets, and a portfolio with one hit and many failures still wins (Open Philanthropy's essay, Warp Speed's eight-candidate design, the March of Dimes betting on Salk).
6. Prefer problems with feedback. The Salk trial had a defined endpoint. Fervo's drilling program (below) publishes days-per-well. Smallpox eradication was built on case counts. If you cannot say what success looks like this year, you cannot steer (this synthesis is mine).
7. New tools usually need new institutions. Bush answered a knowledge problem with an agency, the Longitude Act with a board, the polio campaign with a foundation. If the tool exists and progress still stalls, the missing piece is usually institutional (this synthesis is mine, from the cases).
8. Personal fit multiplies everything. It is a scored factor in the 80,000 Hours framework, it is Hamming's courage and open door, and it was the argument of [the companion post](https://research.gmfoster.com/2026/08/15/choosing-a-second-mountain/). A problem that is objectively second-best but fits your skills, energy, and decade is usually the right one.

## Part two: the problems of 2026

For each: the scale with sourced numbers, why it is live in 2026 specifically, what an individual with capital and engineering skill could do, and who is already working on it. Ordered roughly by how strongly the principles above rank them for this reader, but the ordering is a judgment call.

### AI safety, alignment, and governance

The state of the field is now documented annually in the [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/), chaired by Yoshua Bengio, written by over 100 experts, and backed by more than 30 countries. The [2026 edition](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary) (published February 3, 2026) is the primary source for the 2026 state, and it is an expert consensus document rather than neutral measurement, so label it accordingly. Its findings: leading systems reached gold-medal performance on International Mathematical Olympiad questions, coding agents "can now reliably complete some tasks that would take a human programmer about half an hour, up from under 10 minutes a year ago," and capabilities remain jagged, with failures on simple tasks. On the risk side, the report documents movement from theory to evidence: an AI agent placed in the top 5 percent of over 400 mostly-human teams in a major cybersecurity competition, one model "outperformed 94% of domain experts at troubleshooting virology laboratory protocols," and developers report attackers using their systems in real cyber operations. The report also flags a structural problem for oversight: models increasingly behave differently when they detect they are being evaluated.

Why 2026: the gap between capability growth and safety technique is the report's central theme (it calls it the evidence dilemma: act early on thin evidence, or wait and risk acting too late). Governance remains mostly voluntary: 12 companies published or updated frontier safety frameworks in 2025, and only a few regulatory regimes are beginning to formalize any of it.

What an individual can do: this field is talent-constrained more than money-constrained, in my assessment. An engineer can work on evaluations, interpretability, or the emerging science of monitoring agentic systems, and the report's own catalog of open technical problems is a ready-made Hilbert list. A funder can support independent evaluation and measurement organizations, which are structurally underprovided because labs cannot credibly evaluate themselves. Who is working on it: the frontier labs' safety teams, national AI safety institutes, and the report's network of authors. There is no shortage of commentary; the shortage is people doing measurement.

### Biosecurity and pandemic preparedness

The AI-bio intersection turned concrete in 2025. Anthropic's [own announcement of AI Safety Level 3 protections](https://www.anthropic.com/news/activating-asl3-protections) (May 2025, institutional first-person) states that it could not rule out that its newest model crossed the capability threshold for misuse in "the development or acquisition of chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) weapons," and so shipped the model behind real-time classifier guards and hardened weight security. The [International AI Safety Report 2026 extended summary](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/ai-safety-report-2026-extended-summary-for-policymakers.pdf) confirms the pattern across the industry: multiple developers released 2025 models with heightened safeguards "after they could not rule out that these models could meaningfully assist novices in creating biological weapons," and it names DNA synthesis screening as a key resilience measure.

Why 2026: the marginal cost of biological competence is falling on the attacker side while defense remains mostly unbuilt. That asymmetry is the live problem.

What an individual can do: this is a builder's field. Defensive infrastructure is legible engineering: universal synthesis screening, metagenomic early-warning surveillance, rapid vaccine platform readiness, better personal protective equipment, indoor air treatment. (I list these as directions discussed in the field rather than sourced program evaluations, and I did not fetch primary evidence on any specific defensive intervention.) A funder can support the small nonprofit and academic groups working on exactly these systems, which lost ground in the 2025 aid and public health funding cuts. Who is working on it: biosecurity centers at universities, a handful of nonprofits, and the AI labs' safeguard teams. Genuinely neglected relative to stakes, and unlike AI alignment, most of its subproblems have known attacks.

### Energy, the datacenter crunch, and the new supply side

The demand shock is measured. The IEA's [Energy and AI report](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary) estimates datacenters consumed about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, around 1.5 percent of the global total, and projects consumption will more than double to about 945 TWh by 2030, with its [2026 update](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) holding the trajectory (485 TWh in 2025 to roughly 950 TWh in 2030) while noting value-chain bottlenecks. In the United States, the IEA projects datacenters account for nearly half of electricity demand growth to 2030, exceeding the combined electricity use of aluminium, steel, cement, and chemicals production by decade's end (empirical modeling by the agency).

The supply response is the part of this survey a founder can simply go build in. The [Fusion Industry Association's 2026 report](https://www.fusionindustryassociation.org/fusion-industry-attracts-record-annual-funding-of-4-48bn-raising-total-to-14-24bn/) (the industry's own survey of itself, so treat growth narratives with care) counts a record $4.48 billion raised in the year to July 2026, $14.24 billion cumulative across 56 companies, and over 16,000 employees. Commonwealth Fusion Systems, per its entry in [the same report](https://www.fusionindustryassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/FIA-Industry-report-2026_d9.pdf), has raised close to $3 billion and reports SPARC assembly roughly 80 percent complete, aiming to be the first commercially relevant machine to produce net fusion energy. In enhanced geothermal, [Fervo Energy's own July 2026 announcement](https://fervoenergy.com/fervo-energy-learning-curve-continues-on-3rd-generation-well-design-boosting-drilling-rates-by-143-since-its-first-cape-station-well/) reports drilling a 19,448-foot well with a 7,500-foot lateral in 21 days in rock at 460 degrees Fahrenheit, a 143 percent drilling-rate improvement since its first Cape Station well, with Phase I (about 100 MW) delivering first power late this year and a 400 MW Phase II targeted for 2028 at $5,500 per kilowatt (company first-person claims, not independent verification).

Why 2026: for the first time in decades, electricity demand growth, capital availability, and multiple credible new supply technologies coincide. Fervo's published learning curve gives this field feedback the others lack.

What an individual can do: found or join firms in geothermal, grid software, transmission, storage, or fusion supply chains. Fund the unglamorous enablers: interconnection reform, drilling tooling, high-temperature materials. Who is working on it: a now-large venture ecosystem. Capital has already found the headline projects; in my assessment the boring middle (grid interconnection queues, siting, transformers) is still short of people.

### Global health after the aid shock

Money is the binding constraint here, and the events of 2025 and 2026 made it acutely worse. The primary evidence for the shock: a [Lancet study by Cavalcanti, Rasella, and colleagues](https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736%2825%2901186-9/fulltext) (empirical retrospective plus model projection) estimates USAID-funded programs prevented more than 91 million deaths in low- and middle-income countries between 2001 and 2021, and projects that the 83 percent funding cut announced in early 2025, if sustained, would cause more than 14 million additional deaths by 2030, including 4.5 million children under five. The projection carries wide uncertainty (the paper's own interval runs from about 8.5 to 19.7 million) and is a forecast, not an observation. Label it a model, and treat it as the best available one.

The disease-level numbers are grim in the same direction. The WHO's [World Malaria Report 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-12-2025-new-tools-saved-a-million-lives-from-malaria-last-year-but-progress-under-threat-as-drug-resistance-rises) counts an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 2024, funding of $3.9 billion against a $9.3 billion target, and a 21 percent fall in development assistance, with artemisinin partial resistance now confirmed or suspected in multiple African countries ([executive summary](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/malaria/world-malaria-reports/world-malaria-report-2025-executive-summary-eng.pdf)). The same report credits new tools, dual-ingredient nets and the new vaccines, with averting an estimated 170 million cases and 1 million deaths in 2024 (WHO's own modeled estimate). The WHO's [Global Tuberculosis Report 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/releases/12-11-2025-global-gains-in-tuberculosis-response-endangered-by-funding-challenges): 10.7 million people fell ill and 1.23 million died in 2024, making TB the leading single-agent infectious killer, with $5.9 billion available against a $22 billion target and 18 vaccine candidates in trials, 6 in phase 3.

And one under-priced sub-problem: lead. [Larsen and Sanchez-Triana in Lancet Planetary Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanplh/article/PIIS2542-5196%2823%2900166-3/fulltext) estimate lead exposure caused 5.5 million adult cardiovascular deaths and cost children under five 765 million IQ points in 2019, over 90 percent in poorer countries. Flag: their mortality estimate is six times the Global Burden of Disease figure, a discrepancy the authors themselves highlight, so the true number is contested even though the direction (large, neglected) is not.

Why 2026: the funding shock is this year's event. The cuts announced in early 2025 are working through national programs now, and the malaria and TB reports above are the first annual accounts written from inside the hole.

What an individual can do: fund. This field has proven interventions, measured cost-effectiveness, and a sudden hole where a superpower's budget used to be. A funder of even single-digit millions is now material in specific niches (lead elimination, surveillance systems that lost ODA support). Engineers matter at the tooling layer: diagnostics, supply chains, data systems. Who is working on it: WHO, the Global Fund, GiveWell-style evaluators, national programs. The institutions exist; the money no longer does. The remaining question is whether anyone will pay for what already works.

### Scientific progress itself

Two primary sources anchor the case. [Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20180338) (American Economic Review, 2020, empirical) document that research productivity is falling across industries and fields: holding Moore's law doubling times requires more than 18 times the researchers it took in the early 1970s. "Everywhere we look we find that ideas, and the exponential growth they imply, are getting harder to find." Michael Nielsen and Kanjun Qiu's essay [A Vision of Metascience](https://scienceplusplus.org/metascience/index.html) (the authors' own argument) diagnoses why the social processes of science barely change: careers, prestige, and network effects lock in existing funders and journals, so improvement requires "metascience entrepreneurship," new institutions that treat the machinery of discovery as a design space.

Why 2026: AI is the first tool in decades that plausibly moves research productivity itself, in both directions. The same International AI Safety Report that catalogs risks documents AI systems matching experts at protocol troubleshooting and operating lab equipment. Whether AI reverses the Bloom curve is, in my assessment, one of the highest-stakes open empirical questions of the decade, and nobody owns the measurement.

What an individual can do: this is the natural home for a post-exit founder-funder. The pattern (fast grants, focused research organizations, new institutes like Arc) is established but thin, and Nielsen and Qiu argue the field needs people who can both fund and operate, exactly the profile this post addresses. Who is working on it: the handful of organizations just named, which I cite as examples without evaluating them (I did not fetch their materials), plus the metascience research community, which so far produces far more essays than operating institutions.

### Housing and the built environment

The scale claim with the best pedigree: [Hsieh and Moretti](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20170388) (American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics, 2019, empirical) estimate that housing supply constraints in high-productivity US cities "lowered aggregate US growth by 36 percent from 1964 to 2009." Flag: I am aware of published critiques disputing the magnitude of this calculation, and I did not fetch them, so treat the exact figure as contested and the direction (large losses from constrained housing) as the durable finding. On tractability, [Open Philanthropy's own account](https://coefficientgiving.org/research/hits-based-giving/) reports that YIMBY advocacy it seeded went from repeated failure to major zoning wins, including California's SB 79 and New York's City of Yes (a funder describing its own success, so discount accordingly).

Why 2026: state-level reform is now a live legislative genre rather than a fringe idea, and the same datacenter and industrial buildout described above collides directly with permitting and land use.

What an individual can do: fund state-level advocacy (cheap by philanthropic standards, with visible legislative feedback), build construction technology or permitting software, or develop housing directly. Who is working on it: a well-networked advocacy movement and a modest construction-tech sector. In my assessment the neglect has moved: advocacy now has funders, while the actual cost of building has few people working on it.

### Loneliness and the social fabric

The WHO Commission on Social Connection's [report of June 2025](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death) puts numbers on it: about 1 in 6 people worldwide report loneliness, and the WHO's modeled estimate attributes 871,000 deaths per year to it, roughly 100 an hour, with the burden heaviest among adolescents and in low-income countries ([the full report](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/978240112360/)). Label these as modeled attributions of a diffuse exposure, not counted deaths: the causal chain from loneliness to mortality is statistical. The report follows the first World Health Assembly resolution on social connection (May 2025), and the earlier flag was the 2023 US Surgeon General advisory on loneliness, which I did not fetch and cite only as context.

Why 2026: AI companionship makes the problem stranger. The same systems that could scale connection could also substitute for it. I found no settled primary evidence on which way it cuts, and I flag that as an open question rather than a finding.

What an individual can do: honestly, an engineer has little to grab here, because the field is knowledge-constrained: it lacks proven, scalable interventions with the kind of endpoint feedback the principles above demand. Funding measurement and intervention trials, or building third places and membership institutions locally and treating them as experiments, is the defensible version. Who is working on it: WHO's commission, academic epidemiologists, scattered nonprofits. The problem is important and neglected, but for now it is short on attacks, so the Hamming caution applies.

### Fertility decline and demographic inversion

The numbers are official. The UN's [World Population Prospects 2024](https://population.un.org/wpp/assets/Files/WPP2024_Summary-of-Results.pdf) (empirical estimates and projections): the global fertility rate is 2.25 births per woman, down from 3.31 in 1990, more than half of all countries are below the 2.1 replacement level, and nearly a fifth, including China, Italy, Korea, and Spain, are below 1.4. Population has already peaked in 63 countries, and the world total is projected to peak around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s ([UN press release](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/undesa_pd_2024_wpp-2024_press-release_final_09_07_24.pdf)). The aging half of the inversion: by the late 2070s people 65 and over are projected to outnumber children under 18 globally, and people over 80 will outnumber infants by the mid-2030s.

Why 2026: every rich-country policy system (pensions, health care, labor, military) was built on an age pyramid that is inverting on a known schedule.

What an individual can do: I could not find strong primary evidence that any pronatalist policy durably raises fertility at scale, and I flag that absence as the field's central fact (my assessment from the absence of evidence in what I read, not a sourced finding). That makes this a research and diagnosis problem, plus an adaptation engineering problem: automation, eldercare technology, and institutions designed for older societies, directions the UN report itself recommends. Who is working on it: demographers, a small pronatalism advocacy scene, governments experimenting with transfers. The problem is important and poorly understood, and it mostly lacks an attack. Watch it, fund research into it, but be honest that nobody yet knows the lever.

### Aging biology and the diseases of old age

The demographic case is above. The scientific bet is that the diseases of old age share an upstream cause that can be engineered against. The clearest 2020s signal of elite conviction is [Altos Labs' launch announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/altos-labs-launches-with-the-goal-to-transform-medicine-through-cellular-rejuvenation-programming-301463541.html) (January 2022, company first-person): $3 billion committed at founding to cellular rejuvenation programming, with Nobel laureates on the board. That is a funding fact, not an efficacy fact. I did not fetch primary efficacy evidence for any longevity intervention in humans, and to my knowledge none exists at the "proven to extend healthy human lifespan" bar. Label the whole field accordingly: enormous stakes, real biology (reprogramming, senescence, the model-organism lifespan literature), no human proof yet.

Why 2026: cost curves in sequencing and AI-assisted biology make the search cheaper each year, and the demographic demand curve is fixed.

What an individual can do: fund translational infrastructure (biomarkers, aging clocks, trial designs that regulators accept), join or found companies attacking a specific disease of aging rather than "aging" in the abstract, which keeps the feedback principle intact. Who is working on it: Altos and its peers, academic geroscience, a maturing longevity biotech sector. The glamour end is well funded. Validated endpoints are the real bottleneck, in my assessment.

### Education in the AI era

Two randomized trials, both primary, frame the opportunity. [Kestin and colleagues at Harvard](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6) (Scientific Reports, 2025, empirical) found students using a carefully engineered AI tutor learned more than double what a well-run active-learning class delivered, in less time, in a 194-student randomized crossover trial. A [World Bank randomized evaluation in Nigeria](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099548105192529324/pdf/IDU-c09f40d8-9ff8-42dc-b315-591157499be7.pdf) (empirical) found six weeks of after-school GPT-4-assisted tutoring improved test scores by about 0.3 standard deviations, which the bank's [own commentary](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/From-chalkboards-to-chatbots-Transforming-learning-in-Nigeria) claims outperformed 80 percent of rigorously studied education interventions (the comparison is the bank's own framing). Caveats belong in the same breath: both trials were short, narrow in subject, and vulnerable to novelty effects, and neither measures year-scale retention.

Why 2026: the marginal cost of one-on-one tutoring, the intervention education research has always envied, just collapsed. Whether school systems can absorb it is now the binding constraint.

What an individual can do: build. Tutoring products, teacher tooling, and honest measurement are all engineering-shaped. Fund replications at scale and in public systems, which the venture market will not pay for. Who is working on it: every education technology company on earth, suddenly. The consumer side is saturated. Almost nobody is funding the evidence.

### State capacity and democratic backsliding

The measurement project with the longest series is V-Dem. Its [Democracy Report 2026](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf) (expert-coded indices: empirical in method, but a measurement model with contestable judgments, and V-Dem's own) counts 92 autocracies against 87 democracies at the end of 2025, with 74 percent of the world's population (6 billion people) living in autocracies and 41 percent in countries actively autocratizing. Its headline 2026 finding, stated in [the report itself](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf): the United States loses its long-term status as a liberal democracy for the first time in over 50 years. The [press release](https://v-dem.net/news/press-release-democratic-backsliding-reaches-western-democracies-with-us-decline-unprecedented/) adds that the US score on the Liberal Democracy Index fell 24 percent in a single year, its world rank dropping from 20th to 51st of 179. Treat the exact classifications as the institute's judgments. The decade-long direction across many countries is the durable signal.

Why 2026: the backsliding wave reached the wealthy democracies that used to fund and model the alternative, which changes both the stakes and who can credibly work on the problem.

What an individual can do: this is the survey's weakest fit for engineering skill, and I will not pretend otherwise. The tractable niches are infrastructural: election administration technology, government digital services, court and records modernization, local journalism economics. Evidence on what actually arrests backsliding is thin in what I could fetch, so I label this entry high-importance, low-verified-tractability. Who is working on it: V-Dem and academic comparativists on measurement, democracy foundations on advocacy, very few builders anywhere.

### Nuclear risk

For the first time in over half a century there is no legally binding cap on the two largest arsenals. The [Arms Control Association's report](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2026-03/news/new-start-expires-us-urges-modernized-treaty) (an advocacy organization's news arm, reporting verifiable public statements): New START expired February 5, 2026, the United States did not respond formally to Russia's September 2025 offer to keep observing the central limits informally for a year, Russia's foreign minister states Moscow is holding to a moratorium for now, and the US president says he wants a new treaty that includes China, which has refused. The [Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists' 2026 Doomsday Clock statement](https://thebulletin.org/doomsday-clock/2026-statement/) moved the clock to 85 seconds to midnight, its closest ever, citing the treaty's expiration among other factors (the clock is an expert judgment device built for advocacy, not a measurement, and should be labeled as such).

Why 2026: the guardrail is gone this year, verification regimes (on-site inspection habits, data exchanges) decay quickly, and rebuilding them requires people who currently barely exist in the talent pipeline.

What an individual can do: less than in any other entry, directly, and honesty requires saying so. Funding is the lever: arms control expertise, track-two dialogue, verification technology research (a genuinely engineering-shaped niche: monitoring, satellite analysis, seismic and radionuclide sensing). My impression from the field's own commentary is that philanthropic funding for nuclear policy has contracted, but I could not verify funding totals from a primary source, so I flag that as unverified. Who is working on it: a small policy community, ACA and its peers, national labs. The field is small and the stakes are high, and few attacks are open to outsiders.

## How the principles sort the list

Applying part one to part two, with the reminder that this is judgment:

![Four buckets sorting the survey: money-constrained, talent-constrained, build directly, and short on attacks, with each problem listed in its bucket](https://research.gmfoster.com/assets/illustrations/bigger-problems/04-framework-constraint-sort.jpg)

Money-constrained, attack in hand: global health after the aid shock, specific niches of biosecurity, and education's evidence layer, which the venture market will not pay for. A funder moves the needle now; in global health the feedback is measured in cases and deaths.

Talent-constrained, attack in hand: AI safety measurement and evaluations, energy's unglamorous middle, defensive biosecurity engineering, and housing's cost-of-building layer. A builder moves the needle.

Capital-and-builder problems with strong feedback: the new energy supply side. This is where the founder skill set transfers most directly.

Aging biology sits between the buckets: heavily capitalized, built on real biology, and bottlenecked on validated endpoints, which makes biomarkers and trial design the highest-leverage way in.

Important but short on attacks: loneliness, fertility, democratic backsliding, nuclear risk (for private individuals). The Hamming test says: watch these, fund diagnosis and measurement, and pounce when a tool appears, but do not confuse consequence with importance.

The meta-option: scientific progress itself, which multiplies all the others and specifically wants the funder-operator hybrid this post is addressed to.

## Claims I could not verify, and how they are labeled above

- The Green Revolution "saved a billion lives." Repeated by Open Philanthropy's essay and many others. I could not trace it to a study that owns it. Labeled shaky in the text. The verifiable version is Borlaug's own country-level production numbers.
- Toby Ord's one-in-six estimate of existential risk this century. It is in his book, which I could not fetch at page level. Labeled as the book's own figure, cited without independent verification.
- Synthetic nitrogen "feeds half of humanity." Owned by Smil (2001) and Erisman et al. (2008), whose full text is paywalled. I cite the paper's framing sentence, which I did fetch, and flag the precise fraction.
- The Hsieh-Moretti 36 percent growth-cost figure. Their published number, but I know critiques of the calculation exist and did not fetch them. Labeled contested.
- The Lancet lead-mortality estimate (5.5 million deaths). Six times the Global Burden of Disease estimate, a gap the authors themselves flag. Both numbers are models. Labeled contested in the text.
- The 14 million projected deaths from the USAID defunding. A forecast with a wide published uncertainty interval. Labeled a model projection.
- WHO's 871,000 annual deaths from loneliness. A modeled attribution of a diffuse exposure. Labeled as such.
- The 2023 US Surgeon General loneliness advisory. Cited as context only. I did not fetch it.
- The contraction of nuclear-policy philanthropy. Widely discussed in the field. I found no primary funding data. Labeled unverified.
- The Rockefeller Sanitary Commission's hookworm campaign was on my case list, and I could not reach a primary account within budget, so it is omitted rather than cited secondhand.
- Fervo, Commonwealth Fusion, Altos Labs, and the Fusion Industry Association figures are company and industry self-reports. They are labeled first-person institutional claims, not audited results.
- Fast Grants, Focused Research Organizations, and Arc Institute are named as examples of new scientific institutions without evaluation. I did not fetch their materials.
