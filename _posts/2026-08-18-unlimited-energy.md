## Generation is growing, but delivery remains scarce

Empirical finding: Current energy systems remain far from literal abundance and are in the early stages of a historically unusual expansion of electricity. The International Energy Agency, or IEA, estimates that total energy demand grew by 8 exajoules in 2025 after reaching nearly 650 EJ in 2024, which puts the 2025 total near 658 EJ. That is an average power flow of about 20.9 terawatts. Electricity consumption was about 28,600 terawatt-hours, or 3.3 TW on average. In 2025 the world added a record 800 gigawatts of renewable capacity, 75 percent of it solar, plus almost 110 GW of battery storage. Solar generation alone rose by 600 TWh, the largest one-year increase ever recorded for any source outside post-crisis rebounds ([IEA 2025 global trends](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2025/global-trends), [IEA 2026 global trends](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/global-trends), [IEA 2026 key findings](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings), [IEA Electricity Mid-Year Update 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-mid-year-update-2026/executive-summary)).

Empirical inference: An energy-intensive project requires *delivered, firm, usable energy at a particular place and time*, not an abstract annual total. A cheap midday megawatt-hour in an uncongested desert cannot run a Tokyo factory at midnight, propel a vehicle in low Earth orbit, or heat a kiln unless generation is joined to transmission, storage, conversion equipment, and a customer. Generation is becoming cheaper and easier to manufacture. The binding constraints are shifting toward grids, interconnection, firming, project finance, siting, and the machines that turn electricity into useful work ([IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA transmission](https://www.iea.org/reports/building-the-future-transmission-grid/executive-summary), [IEA investment](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

Empirical calculation: A literal 10x increase over 2025 total energy is about 6,580 EJ per year, or 209 TW continuously; a literal 100x increase is 65,800 EJ, or 2.09 PW. These targets exceed ordinary infrastructure planning. By comparison, 10x today's electricity is 286,000 TWh per year. That target would still require a very large buildout of power plants, factories, cables, mines, and permits, but it can be specified in those components. Electrification can also provide the same service with less primary energy than combustion ([IEA electrification](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/electricity/electrification), [EIA on conversion losses](https://www.eia.gov/Todayinenergy/detail.php?id=44436)).

Empirical inference: A 10x increase in electricity would be a hard, multidecade industrial mission, while a 10x increase in total energy would require rebuilding much of the physical economy around electricity and new fuels. Raising total energy use on Earth by 100x would also encounter land, materials, governance, and possible heat-disposal constraints. None of these energy scenarios violates known physics, unlike matter teleportation: the canonical experiment transfers a quantum state through entanglement and classical communication, destroys the original state, and does not permit instantaneous information transfer ([Bennett et al., 1993](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.70.1895)).

## What "energy" means

Empirical finding: Energy statistics contain several different objects. Total energy supply, or TES, counts energy entering an economy before transformation; the IEA defines it as production plus imports, minus exports and international bunkers, adjusted for stock changes. Total final consumption counts energy reaching end-use sectors, while electricity is a secondary carrier. Capacity is maximum instantaneous output in watts, generation is accumulated output in watt-hours, and capacity factor is actual generation divided by maximum possible generation over the same period ([IEA glossary](https://www.iea.org/glossary), [EIA electricity basics](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/index.php), [EIA capacity and generation](https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=101&t=3)).

Empirical finding: The freshest detailed official world balance I found is for 2022. The United Nations reports 619.2 EJ of world total energy supply: 187.5 EJ oil, 172.3 EJ coal, 145.7 EJ natural gas, 52.4 EJ biofuels and waste, 29.0 EJ nuclear, and 32.2 EJ in its electricity and heat category. Fossil fuels therefore supplied 505.5 EJ, or 81.6 percent. Final consumption was 426.0 EJ, including 88.4 EJ of electricity. Industry consumed 130.7 EJ, transport 111.8 EJ, and households 85.1 EJ, excluding non-energy uses where applicable ([UN Energy Statistics Pocketbook 2025, pages 8-9 and 43-45](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf)).

Empirical interpretation: The 193 EJ gap between supply and final consumption is not a pile of energy waiting to be claimed. Energy balances include conversion losses, energy-sector use, and distribution losses. Thermal generation discards substantial heat, while an electric motor, electric vehicle, or heat pump can deliver the same service with less final or primary energy than a combustion alternative. The numerical value assigned to noncombustible primary electricity also depends on the accounting convention ([EIA on conversion losses](https://www.eia.gov/Todayinenergy/detail.php?id=44436), [EIA on renewable accounting methods](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=41013), [IEA electrification](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/electricity/electrification)).

Empirical inference: "Ten times energy" is therefore an ambiguous mission. Ten times useful services, such as mobility, comfortable buildings, computation, clean water, and industrial output, might require far less than 10x primary energy if the system electrifies and becomes more efficient. Conversely, making aviation fuel, steel, fertilizer, and round-the-clock electricity from clean power can require much more generation than today's final electricity consumption. A serious abundance goal should specify a service level, location, reliability, carbon intensity, and price, not just exajoules ([IEA electrification](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/electricity/electrification), [UN 2022 world balance](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf)).

## Two meanings of 10x and 100x

Empirical calculation: These scale calculations, not forecasts, use 658 EJ per year as the approximate 2025 total-energy baseline and 28,600 TWh as 2025 electricity consumption. One year is 8,760 hours, and one TWh is 0.0036 EJ ([IEA 2025 global trends](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2025/global-trends), [IEA 2026 global trends](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/global-trends), [IEA Electricity Mid-Year Update 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-mid-year-update-2026/executive-summary)).

| Scenario | Annual energy | Average power | Ratio to 2025 electricity |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 total energy | about 658 EJ | 20.9 TW | 6.4 times |
| 2025 electricity consumption | 28,600 TWh, or 103 EJ | 3.3 TW | 1 time |
| 10x electricity | 286,000 TWh, or 1,030 EJ | 32.6 TW | 10 times |
| 100x electricity | 2,860,000 TWh, or 10,296 EJ | 326 TW | 100 times |
| Literal 10x total energy | 6,580 EJ, equivalent to 1.83 million TWh | 209 TW | 63.9 times |
| Literal 100x total energy | 65,800 EJ, equivalent to 18.3 million TWh | 2.09 PW | 639 times |

Empirical inference: The most useful near-term mission is 10x clean electricity, not 10x primary energy. It is measurable, compatible with electrification, and large enough to make hydrogen, synthetic fuels, desalination, carbon removal, and new industry consequential. Literal 100x total energy is a different category. It implies energy throughput more than six hundred times present electricity consumption and would make planetary heat flows and land transformation part of the engineering brief ([IEA electrification](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/electricity/electrification), [IPCC energy-balance assessment](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-7/), [NREL solar land study](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy13osti/56290.pdf)).

## A solar-only yardstick, not a proposal

Empirical finding: IRENA reports that new utility-scale solar PV commissioned in 2024 had a global weighted-average installed cost of $691 per kilowatt, a levelized cost of $0.043 per kWh, and a 17.4 percent capacity factor. In 2025, solar accounted for about 600 GW of the record 800 GW of renewable capacity additions ([IRENA Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024), [IEA 2026 key findings](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings)).

Empirical calculation: If solar alone supplied the incremental energy for 10x electricity, with no efficiency gain and a constant 17.4 percent capacity factor, the world would need about 169 TW of new solar nameplate capacity. At $691 per kW, generation equipment and installation alone would cost about $117 trillion. Spread evenly across 25 years, that is $4.7 trillion per year, before storage, transmission, distribution, financing, replacements, or customer equipment. The required capacity equals about 281 cohorts of the solar capacity installed in 2025. This is a ratio, not a 281-year forecast, because manufacturing can grow ([IRENA 2024 costs](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024), [IEA 2026 additions](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings)).

Empirical calculation: The equivalent solar-only yardstick for 100x electricity is about 1.86 petawatts of new capacity and $1.28 quadrillion of generation investment. Spread across 25 years, the required $51 trillion per year compares with projected 2025 global energy investment of $3.3 trillion, including $2.2 trillion for clean technologies and $1.1 trillion for fossil fuels ([IEA World Energy Investment 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

Empirical calculation: An older NREL study estimated an average total land-use requirement of 3.6 acres per annual GWh for US utility-scale PV. Applying that US value mechanically produces about 3.75 million square kilometers for the incremental generation in the 10x-electricity case and 41.25 million square kilometers for 100x. Rooftops, higher-efficiency modules, agrivoltaics, offshore wind, nuclear, and transmission from better resources would all alter that footprint. This one-technology extrapolation is only a stress test and should not be read as a build plan ([NREL, Land-Use Requirements for Solar Power Plants in the United States](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy13osti/56290.pdf)).

## Cheap generation is not cheap energy service

Empirical finding: IRENA found that 91 percent of utility-scale renewable capacity commissioned in 2024 produced electricity more cheaply, on a levelized basis, than the cheapest new fossil-fuel alternative. Its global averages were $34/MWh for onshore wind and $43/MWh for utility solar. Those figures describe plant-level lifetime generation cost, not the cost of delivering a balanced megawatt-hour through a constrained grid at the instant a customer wants it ([IRENA 2024 costs](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024)).

Empirical finding: The grid is already lagging generation. The IEA estimates that the world must add or refurbish more than 80 million kilometers of grids by 2040, roughly the size of the existing global grid. At least 3,000 GW of renewable projects were waiting in connection queues in 2023. Grid investment was about $400 billion in 2025 while generation investment approached $1 trillion. The IEA says grid spending needs to rise toward parity with generation spending early in the 2030s ([IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA investment](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

Empirical finding: Timing compounds the problem. New renewable projects can take one to five years, while new grid infrastructure often takes five to fifteen. Cable procurement can take two to three years, large transformers up to four, and some high-voltage direct-current cables more than five. By 2024, about 1,650 GW of advanced solar and wind projects were awaiting grid connections. Permitting was the largest source of transmission delay in many jurisdictions ([IEA, Building the Future Transmission Grid](https://www.iea.org/reports/building-the-future-transmission-grid/executive-summary)).

Empirical finding: Storage solves hours more readily than seasons. The IEA describes today's batteries as best suited to one-to-eight-hour flexibility. Most systems added in 2025 had roughly two hours of duration, though four-hour systems were growing. In an energy-transition scenario consistent with tripling renewables, global storage capacity would need to grow sixfold to 1,500 GW by 2030, including 1,200 GW of batteries. The US Department of Energy's long-duration program targets a levelized storage cost of $0.05/kWh for systems lasting more than ten hours, a target rather than a description of current commercial supply ([IEA batteries](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA battery storage in 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-battery-storage), [DOE Long Duration Storage Shot](https://www.energy.gov/oe/articles/new-report-showcases-how-innovation-can-fast-track-affordable-energy-storage)).

Empirical inference: Cost, volume, location, timing, and finance jointly determine whether energy can serve a load. Cheap bulk generation expands the number of possible uses, but volume limits how many can run at once, location determines whether power can reach them, temporal availability determines reliability, and finance determines whether the connecting system is built. For many future loads, the binding metric will be the cost of firm power at the customer's busbar, including grid and conversion equipment, rather than the generator's levelized cost ([IRENA 2024 generation costs](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024), [IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA storage](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary)).

## Constraints beyond generation

Empirical finding, generation and manufacturing: Annual renewable additions reached 800 GW in 2025, but a 10x-electricity solar yardstick requires nearly 169,000 GW of incremental capacity. Fission supplied 377.08 GW of net operable capacity at the end of 2025, while construction began on 12.2 GW during the year. Even at a hypothetical 90 percent capacity factor, supplying the 10x-electricity increment with nuclear alone would require about 32.6 TW, roughly 87 times the existing fleet ([IEA 2026 key findings](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings), [IAEA PRIS world statistics](https://pris.iaea.org/PRIS/WorldStatistics/WorldTrendNuclearPowerCapacity.aspx), [IEA nuclear in 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-nuclear)).

Empirical finding, materials: Critical-mineral supply is not a fixed geological ceiling, but concentration and project lead time create chokepoints. The IEA reports that the average share of the leading refining country across key minerals, excluding rare earths, rose to 72 percent in 2025. Its project pipeline implies a possible 25 percent copper supply deficit by 2035, while investment in critical-mineral mining fell 9 percent in 2025. Prices can induce substitution, recycling, and new mines, but not instantly ([IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary)).

Empirical finding, interconnection: The United States had 2,061 GW of proposed generation and storage in interconnection queues at the end of 2025. For projects entering US queues from 2000 through 2020, only 13 percent of requested capacity reached operation and 75 percent was withdrawn. Median time from request to commercial operation now exceeds five years. A queue is not a project pipeline, but it is direct evidence that permission and network studies can bottleneck equipment that developers would like to build ([Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up 2026](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/backlog-power-plants-seeking-transmission-grid-connection-eased-somewhat-2025-amidst)).

Empirical finding, finance: Capital scarcity is local even when global savings are ample. The IEA projects $2.2 trillion of clean-energy investment in 2025 but notes that Africa, with about 20 percent of world population, receives only 2 percent of clean-energy investment. In many emerging markets, debt service accounts for about 85 percent of total energy-project investment costs. A lower module price does not cure a high cost of capital, a weak utility balance sheet, or currency risk ([IEA World Energy Investment 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

Empirical finding, fuels and heat: Electricity was only 88.4 EJ of 426.0 EJ of final consumption in the UN's 2022 balance. Oil alone was 168.5 EJ. A power-abundance mission must therefore build not only generators but also electric vehicles, heat pumps, furnaces, electrolyzers, fuel synthesis, and redesigned industrial processes. The IEA finds that commercially available heat pumps could technically serve about 20 percent of industrial heat demand, mainly below 120 C, while higher-temperature applications remain at earlier stages ([UN Energy Statistics Pocketbook 2025](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf), [IEA Heat Pump Monitor 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/heat-pump-monitor-2026/key-findings)).

Empirical inference, siting and political economy: At 10x scale, siting technical components raises land-use and legitimacy issues. Transmission corridors cross jurisdictions, while mines, reservoirs, reactors, turbines, and solar fields concentrate local costs even when benefits extend beyond host regions. Faster permitting can reduce delay, but a durable system also requires compensation, community participation, credible safety institutions, and consent where rights apply. Because permitting is a leading transmission delay and energy investment is geographically unequal, system design must account for distribution ([IEA transmission](https://www.iea.org/reports/building-the-future-transmission-grid/executive-summary), [IEA investment](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

## Which technologies can carry the mission?

Empirical assessment, solar, wind, and batteries: These are commercial, modular, and scaling fastest. Renewables generated 34 percent of global electricity in 2025, with wind and solar together at 17 percent. Further deployment depends on the pace of grid expansion, flexible demand, short-duration batteries, longer-duration storage, and firm low-carbon backup, rather than on whether individual panels or turbines work. The IEA expects renewable generation to rise from 9,900 TWh in 2024 to 16,200 TWh in 2030 under its main forecast, a major expansion that still reaches only about half of today's total generation ([IEA 2026 electricity supply](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/electricity-supply), [IEA Renewables 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2025/renewable-electricity)).

Empirical assessment, fission: Nuclear fission is commercial firm low-carbon power, but the current fleet is small beside a 10x target and new construction is capital intensive. The IEA's stated-policy case raises global nuclear capacity by more than 50 percent to nearly 650 GW in 2050. That is meaningful for decarbonization and system firmness, but it is less than 2 percent of the hypothetical 32.6 TW nuclear capacity needed for the 10x-electricity increment. Fission can be part of an abundance portfolio; current deployment trends do not make it the whole answer ([IEA nuclear investment outlook](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-path-to-a-new-era-for-nuclear-energy/outlook-for-nuclear-investment), [IAEA PRIS](https://pris.iaea.org/PRIS/WorldStatistics/WorldTrendNuclearPowerCapacity.aspx)).

Empirical assessment, geothermal: Conventional geothermal provides firm power but is geographically limited. The IEA estimates geothermal currently meets less than 1 percent of global energy demand. With major improvements in drilling, project development, and cost, next-generation geothermal could reach as much as 800 GW and 6,000 TWh per year by 2050. That would add firm generation, yet only about one fifth of 2025 electricity consumption and a much smaller fraction of a 10x target ([IEA, The Future of Geothermal Energy](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-future-of-geothermal-energy/executive-summary)).

Empirical assessment, fusion: Fusion has crossed a scientific threshold, not a power-plant threshold. At the National Ignition Facility, an April 2025 target produced 8.6 MJ of fusion energy from 2.08 MJ of laser light. The facility's power-conditioning system stores roughly 400 MJ from the grid for a shot, and LLNL says an inertial-fusion power plant would need about one million times NIF's shot rate plus much higher wall-plug efficiency. ITER's revised baseline schedules deuterium-tritium operation for 2039 and aims for 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of plasma heating for 400 to 600 seconds. Neither machine is designed to sell net electricity ([LLNL NIF record](https://lasers.llnl.gov/news/target-breakthrough-enabled-fusion-record-nif), [LLNL power conditioning](https://lasers.llnl.gov/about/how-nif-works/power-conditioning-system), [LLNL on higher power](https://str.llnl.gov/str-march-2026/pursuit-higher-power), [ITER baseline](https://www.iter.org/node/20687/new-baseline-prioritize-robust-start-exploitation)).

Empirical assessment, space-based solar: NASA's 2024 study found substantial capability gaps in autonomous assembly, maintenance, and wireless power beaming. Its two reference systems produced estimated levelized costs of $0.61 and $1.59 per kWh, 12 to 80 times NASA's projected 2050 terrestrial alternatives. One 2 GW design required 2,321 launches and cost $276 billion; the other required 3,960 launches and cost $434 billion. Space solar may fit remote or strategic niches after large advances in launch and orbital construction. On NASA's assumptions it is not a near-term route to cheap bulk terrestrial power ([NASA report page](https://www.nasa.gov/organizations/otps/space-based-solar-power-report/), [NASA technical report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230018600/downloads/OTPS%20SBSP%20Report%20Final_Tagged_Approved_1_5_24.pdf)).

## Uses for abundant electricity

Empirical calculation, AI and compute: Data centers consumed about 415 TWh in 2024 and the IEA projects about 945 TWh in 2030. That projected demand is only 3.3 percent of 2025 world electricity consumption, but its concentration makes it a local grid constraint: half of US data-center capacity is in five regional clusters, typical centers draw 10 to 25 MW, hyperscale centers exceed 100 MW, and announced campuses can reach 5 GW. Ten times electricity would make aggregate compute energy easier to supply, but new substations, transmission, generation, chips, cooling, and water would remain location-specific constraints ([IEA, Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary), [IEA, Understanding the Energy-AI Nexus](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/understanding-the-energy-ai-nexus)).

Empirical calculation, water: A scenario that desalinates 100 liters per day for each of 8.2 billion people would produce about 299 cubic kilometers per year. At 3 kWh per cubic meter, near the 2.75 to 2.98 kWh/m3 total demonstrated by a US Bureau of Reclamation seawater system, it would consume about 898 TWh per year, or 3.1 percent of 2025 electricity. Cheap power makes desalination more available, but intake, brine disposal, pipes, reservoirs, and distance inland remain physical and ecological costs ([UN World Population Prospects 2024](https://www.un.org/en/UN-projects-world-population-to-peak-within-this-century), [US Bureau of Reclamation desalination report](https://www.usbr.gov/research/publications/download_product.cfm?id=706)).

Empirical calculation, hydrogen and synthetic fuels: Global hydrogen demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025, almost all of it supplied through incumbent fossil-based routes. At 50 kWh of electricity per kilogram, an IEA reference value including compression, replacing just that volume with electrolysis would require roughly 5,000 TWh per year, or 17.5 percent of today's electricity. Making hydrocarbons from hydrogen and captured carbon incurs further conversion losses. Abundant clean electricity would make synthetic aviation and shipping fuels plausible at scale, but cheap power alone does not erase electrolyzer, carbon-source, transport, and plant-capital costs ([IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2026 demand](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-hydrogen-review-2026/demand), [IEA on hydrogen terminology and electricity use](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/why-clearer-terminology-for-hydrogen-could-unlock-investment-and-scale-up-production)).

Empirical calculation, carbon removal: An original process design by Keith and coauthors estimated 366 kWh of electricity plus 5.25 GJ of natural gas per tonne of CO2 for one configuration of direct air capture. At one gigatonne per year, that is 366 TWh of electricity and 5.25 EJ of gas, before transport and geologic storage. The paper estimated $94 to $232 per tonne under its assumptions and had authors affiliated with the developer Carbon Engineering, so it is a design estimate with a commercial conflict to disclose, not a fleet average. DOE's Carbon Negative Shot aims for durable removal below $100 per net tonne at gigatonne scale by 2050, explicitly a target rather than present capability ([Keith et al., 2018](https://keith.seas.harvard.edu/files/tkg/files/keith_et_al._-_2018_-_a_process_for_capturing_co2_from_the_atmosphere.pdf), [DOE Carbon Negative Shot](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/carbon-negative-shot-strategy)).

Empirical finding, food and materials: Cheap clean power can lower the energy cost of fertilizer, controlled-environment agriculture, water pumping, recycling, high-temperature heat, and some new material pathways. The often repeated claim that synthetic nitrogen fertilizer sustains about half of humanity traces to a 2008 model by Erisman and coauthors, which estimated the share of world population supported by Haber-Bosch nitrogen. It is a model-based counterfactual, not a head count, and should not be treated as a timeless constant ([Erisman et al., 2008](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo325)).

Empirical calculation, Mars and launch: The ideal kinetic energy of placing 100 tonnes at orbital speed, using 7.8 km/s and ignoring gravity and drag losses, is about 3.0 terajoules or 845 MWh. At $30/MWh, that lower bound costs about $25,000 in energy. NASA's rocket equation shows why launch is difficult: reaching low Earth orbit requires a large propellant mass ratio, and real vehicles must also carry tanks, engines, heat protection, and margins. Cheaper terrestrial energy helps make propellant and hardware, but propulsion performance, vehicle mass, reliability, reuse, orbital refueling, life support, and launch cadence dominate a Mars program long before the commodity price of 845 MWh does ([NASA ideal rocket equation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/ideal-rocket-equation/), [SpaceX Mars mission architecture](https://www.spacex.com/mars)).

## Cheaper energy can increase demand

Traditional wisdom: In 1865, William Stanley Jevons argued that improvements in coal-use efficiency could increase rather than decrease total coal consumption by making coal-powered activity cheaper and more widespread. The proposition now called the Jevons paradox began as an argument about industrial organization, not a universal numerical law ([Jevons, *The Coal Question*](https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html?chapter_num=2)).

Empirical finding: Modern rebound estimates vary by technology, time horizon, and market boundary. A review by Gillingham and coauthors found positive direct rebound effects in many energy services but emphasized that economy-wide estimates are difficult and that evidence for complete "backfire" is not robust enough to assume it everywhere. Falling cost can reduce energy used per service while increasing the number of services purchased ([Gillingham et al., 2014](https://www.rff.org/documents/1036/RFF-DP-14-39.pdf)).

Empirical inference: Rebound does not make abundance undesirable; it means cheap energy alone cannot be assumed to reduce emissions or material throughput. When additional supply is clean, growth in cooling, mobility, computation, water, and industrial output can be an intended benefit. Policy must still price emissions and local harms, protect common resources, and prevent a cheap input from making damaging outputs cheap ([Gillingham et al., 2014](https://www.rff.org/documents/1036/RFF-DP-14-39.pdf)).

## Abundance for whom?

Empirical finding: The world can have abundant generation in aggregate and severe scarcity for people. The 2025 Tracking SDG 7 report estimated that 666 million people lacked basic electricity access in 2023 and 2.1 billion lacked clean cooking. About 85 percent of people without electricity lived in sub-Saharan Africa. The UN's 2022 energy balance shows total supply of 22.1 GJ per person in Africa versus 272.3 GJ in Northern America, a ratio above twelve to one ([WHO, Tracking SDG 7: The Energy Progress Report 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/2025-tracking-sdg7-report), [WHO release](https://www.who.int/news/item/25-06-2025-energy-access-has-improved--yet-international-financial-support-still-needed-to-boost-progress-and-address-disparities), [UN Energy Statistics Pocketbook 2025, page 45](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf)).

Empirical inference: A 10x system concentrated in a few industrial regions can coexist with energy poverty elsewhere. Distribution requires last-mile networks, solvent utilities, affordable appliances, clean cooking equipment, reliable institutions, and financing in local currency. In some places a small reliable system creates more welfare than another gigawatt connected to a saturated market. The mission should therefore track at least two outcomes: total firm clean supply and a floor of affordable energy services for every person ([Tracking SDG 7, 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/2025-tracking-sdg7-report), [IEA investment](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

## Is this more tractable than teleportation?

Empirical finding: Every major component of a 10x-electricity system exists commercially somewhere: generation, high-voltage transmission, batteries, nuclear power, electrolysis, and heat pumps. The central problems are replication, integration, cost, and governance, and the system does not require fusion or space solar. The IEA's existing-policy renewable forecast adds 4,600 GW from 2025 through 2030, direct evidence that terawatt-scale deployment is an operating industrial process rather than a laboratory conjecture ([IEA Renewables 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2025/renewable-electricity), [IEA batteries](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IAEA reactor database](https://pris.iaea.org/PRIS/WorldStatistics/WorldTrendNuclearPowerCapacity.aspx), [IEA hydrogen demand](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-hydrogen-review-2026/demand), [IEA Heat Pump Monitor 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/heat-pump-monitor-2026/key-findings)).

Empirical finding: Quantum teleportation is real but the name is misleading for a Mars analogy. Bennett and coauthors proposed transferring an unknown quantum state using pre-shared entanglement and a classical message. The original state is destroyed, and classical communication prevents faster-than-light transfer. It does not transport a person or bulk matter ([Bennett et al., 1993](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.70.1895)).

Empirical inference: Building ten times as much clean electricity would resemble earlier railroad, electrification, and urbanization programs in its need for capital, standards, repeated construction, political negotiation, and decades of learning. A literal 100x increase in total energy would also face planetary land and heat constraints, while no demonstrated engineering pathway can teleport a person. Energy abundance is therefore feasible as an engineering mission, but its first milestone cannot credibly be "unlimited" energy. It would begin with cheaper clean generation and faster connection and firm delivery, then extend to efficient electrification, clean fuels and heat, and measurement of who receives the resulting services ([IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [NREL land study](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy13osti/56290.pdf), [Bennett et al., 1993](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.70.1895)).

## At 100x, waste heat becomes a design constraint

Empirical calculation: Using NASA's mean Earth radius of 6,371 km gives a surface area of about 5.10 x 10^14 square meters. If all 658 EJ of today's annual energy use were additional heat, spread evenly, it would average 0.041 W/m2. At 10x the value is 0.41 W/m2; at 100x it is 4.09 W/m2. The IPCC's assessed effective radiative forcing from doubled carbon dioxide is 3.93 W/m2, so a literal 100x of additive terrestrial energy would be in the same numerical range. The climate response would not be identical because location, altitude, timing, and feedbacks differ ([NASA Earth radius reference](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160012759/downloads/20160012759.pdf?attachment=true), [IPCC AR6 Working Group I, Chapter 7](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-7/)).

Empirical qualification: That calculation is an upper-bound warning, not a prediction. Solar energy largely intercepts radiation already entering Earth's climate system, so its net forcing depends on changed albedo and where the energy would otherwise have been absorbed or reflected. Fossil, nuclear, and geothermal heat are more directly additive. NASA estimates that Earth absorbs about 240 W/m2 from sunlight on average, which is almost sixty times the literal 100x flow. Available solar energy remains much larger than the literal 100x flow, but at that scale system design would have to account for both the source and disposal of heat ([NASA on Earth's energy budget](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/)).

## Abundance depends on delivery

Empirical inference: Energy systems have begun to achieve generation abundance while delivery remains scarce. Solar, wind, batteries, and electrification are scaling quickly enough to affect the economy, but not enough on their own to provide unlimited energy. Further progress is constrained by cables, transformers, queues, storage duration, mines, factories, skilled labor, finance, land, and permission ([IEA 2026 additions](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings), [IEA transmission](https://www.iea.org/reports/building-the-future-transmission-grid/executive-summary), [IEA critical minerals](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary)).

Empirical inference: Lower prices can unlock demand and investment, while additional volume has little value if it is unavailable at the required place or time. The engineering objective should therefore be *firm clean energy service* at low delivered cost, available where and when needed, with harms and benefits fairly distributed. A 10x increase in global electricity is large but specific enough to plan, whereas a 100x increase in total energy is too broad to guide engineering and would introduce planetary constraints despite being physically possible ([IRENA 2024 costs](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024), [IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IPCC AR6 Chapter 7](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-7/)).

Empirical inference: More energy could support "Don't Die," Mars missions, artificial intelligence, clean water, synthetic fuels, carbon removal, and industrial production, but it would not remove constraints from biology, logistics, materials, institutions, or time. Energy abundance is more tractable than teleportation because its systems already exist; the remaining question is whether they can be built and governed at sufficient scale ([IEA Renewables 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2025/renewable-electricity), [NASA rocket equation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/ideal-rocket-equation/), [Bennett et al., 1993](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.70.1895)).

## Claims I could not verify

I could not verify the following claims:

- A complete, public, line-by-line 2025 global total-energy balance by source that used the same accounting basis as the UN table. The paper therefore uses the UN's 2022 detailed mix and the IEA's 2025 aggregate update, with years labeled rather than spliced.
- A defensible integrated energy budget or settlement cost for a self-sustaining city on Mars. Published mission concepts choose different crews, cargo, propulsion, life support, and local-production assumptions.
- A universal numerical estimate for economy-wide Jevons backfire. The original claim is historical argument, and modern empirical estimates depend strongly on scope and time horizon.
- Current commercial fusion electricity cost, because no fusion plant sells electricity. Target gain, wall-plug energy, repetition rate, plant availability, and net electric output are different measures.
- That space-based solar, fusion, or any present technology will make electricity "too cheap to meter." The primary roadmaps describe targets and capability gaps, not guaranteed delivered prices.
- An ethically neutral welfare value for 10x or 100x energy. The consequences depend on source, use, location, ownership, and distribution.
