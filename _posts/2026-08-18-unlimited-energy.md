## The short answer

Suppose the world produced ten times as much electricity as it does now. Desalinating 100 liters of water per day for every person on Earth would consume about 0.3 percent of that supply ([population](https://www.un.org/en/UN-projects-world-population-to-peak-within-this-century), [demonstrated energy intensity](https://www.usbr.gov/research/publications/download_product.cfm?id=706)). Replacing today's fossil-based hydrogen with electrolysis would take less than 2 percent ([IEA hydrogen demand](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-hydrogen-review-2026/demand), [IEA electricity-use reference](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/why-clearer-terminology-for-hydrogen-could-unlock-investment-and-scale-up-production)). Projected global data-center demand in 2030 would take about one third of 1 percent ([IEA, *Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)).

At that scale, energy would stop being the first objection to many ambitious projects. Clean water, synthetic fuels, carbon removal, high-temperature industry, controlled environments, and very large computing systems would all become easier to contemplate. Some things that look extravagant now would become ordinary industrial loads.

The surprise is that producing the electricity may not be the hardest part. A cheap midday megawatt-hour in a desert cannot run a Tokyo factory at midnight, heat a kiln behind a congested grid, or power a data center waiting five years for a substation. The useful product is energy in the right form, at the right place and time, with enough reliability for the machine consuming it. Generation is one link in that chain.

My conclusion is that 10× clean electricity is a legitimate civilization-scale mission. Much of the core hardware is commercial: generators, high-voltage lines, batteries, reactors, electrolyzers, and heat pumps ([IEA renewable deployment](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2025/renewable-electricity), [IEA batteries](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IAEA reactor database](https://pris.iaea.org/PRIS/WorldStatistics/WorldTrendNuclearPowerCapacity.aspx)). What does not exist is an integrated system at 10× scale, especially one with enough long-duration firming and high-temperature electrification. A literal 100× increase in all energy use belongs to another category. It would bring land, materials, governance, and eventually waste heat into the planetary design brief.

This makes energy abundance much more tractable than teleportation. Generation, transmission, storage, and electric end-use machines all operate commercially today ([IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA batteries](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA electrification](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/electricity/electrification)). We do not know how to transport a person by quantum teleportation, whose real protocol transfers a quantum state, destroys the original state, and still requires an ordinary classical message ([Bennett and colleagues, 1993](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.70.1895)). The energy mission is a scale problem built out of working systems. Teleportation is not.

## What abundant electricity could make ordinary

The useful distinction is whether energy dominates a project's economics or merely supports it. Desalination and hydrogen sit toward the first end. Providing 100 liters of desalinated water per day to each of 8.2 billion people would produce about 299 cubic kilometers per year. At 3 kWh per cubic meter, close to the 2.75 to 2.98 kWh/m3 total demonstrated by a US Bureau of Reclamation system, it would use 898 TWh per year: 3.1 percent of today's electricity and 0.3 percent of a 10× supply. Intake, brine disposal, pipes, reservoirs, and distance inland would remain ([UN World Population Prospects 2024](https://www.un.org/en/UN-projects-world-population-to-peak-within-this-century), [US Bureau of Reclamation desalination report](https://www.usbr.gov/research/publications/download_product.cfm?id=706)). Replacing the more than 100 million tonnes of hydrogen demanded in 2025 would be larger. At the IEA's reference value of 50 kWh per kilogram, including compression, electrolysis would use about 5,000 TWh per year, or 17.5 percent of today's electricity and 1.7 percent of a 10× system. Synthetic aviation and shipping fuels add further conversion losses plus the cost of electrolyzers, carbon supply, plants, and transport ([IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-hydrogen-review-2026/demand), [IEA on hydrogen electricity use](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/why-clearer-terminology-for-hydrogen-could-unlock-investment-and-scale-up-production)).

Carbon removal and compute show two different versions of the same effect. Keith and colleagues estimated 366 kWh of electricity plus 5.25 GJ of natural gas per tonne of CO2 for one direct-air-capture configuration. At one gigatonne per year, that is 366 TWh of electricity and 5.25 EJ of gas before transport and storage. The authors estimated $94 to $232 per tonne, and several were affiliated with Carbon Engineering, so I treat the result as a disclosed design estimate rather than a fleet average. The Department of Energy's goal of durable removal below $100 per net tonne at gigatonne scale by 2050 is also a target, not present capability ([Keith and colleagues, 2018](https://keith.seas.harvard.edu/files/tkg/files/keith_et_al._-_2018_-_a_process_for_capturing_co2_from_the_atmosphere.pdf), [DOE Carbon Negative Shot](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/carbon-negative-shot-strategy)). Data centers, by contrast, consumed about 415 TWh in 2024, and the IEA projects 945 TWh in 2030, only 3.3 percent of 2025 world electricity. Their problem is concentration: half of US capacity sits in five regional clusters, typical centers draw 10 to 25 MW, hyperscale centers exceed 100 MW, and announced campuses reach 5 GW. Aggregate supply can be comfortable while a campus still waits for transmission, substations, chips, cooling, and water ([IEA, *Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary), [IEA, *Understanding the Energy-AI Nexus*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/understanding-the-energy-ai-nexus)).

Food and materials would change in less easily summarized ways. Cheap clean power lowers the energy cost of fertilizer, water pumping, recycling, controlled-environment agriculture, high-temperature heat, and some new material pathways. The familiar claim that synthetic nitrogen fertilizer sustains about half of humanity traces to a 2008 model by Erisman and colleagues. It is a model-based counterfactual, not a head count or timeless constant, but it shows how thoroughly an energy-intensive industrial process can alter the human carrying capacity of land ([Erisman and colleagues, 2008](https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo325)).

Mars and "Don't Die" sit toward the other end. The ideal kinetic energy of accelerating 100 tonnes to 7.8 km/s is about 3.0 terajoules, or 845 MWh. At $30 per MWh, that lower bound costs about $25,000, almost comically small beside the difficulty of orbit. NASA's rocket equation explains why: a launch vehicle must carry propellant to accelerate the propellant it will use later, along with tanks, engines, heat protection, payload, and margins. Vehicle mass, propulsion performance, reliability, reuse, orbital refueling, life support, and cadence dominate before the commodity price of 845 MWh does ([NASA ideal rocket equation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/ideal-rocket-equation/), [SpaceX Mars architecture](https://www.spacex.com/mars)). My interpretation is that longevity has the same shape. More energy can buy computation, controlled environments, manufacturing, and repeated experiments. Energy abundance can remove energy cost as a constraint, but it cannot supply biological insight, safe interventions, clinical evidence, or time.

## Ten times which energy?

The phrase "10× energy" hides several different quantities. Total energy supply counts energy entering an economy before transformation. The IEA defines it as production plus imports, minus exports and international bunkers, adjusted for stock changes. Total final consumption counts what reaches end-use sectors. Electricity is a secondary carrier. Capacity measures maximum instantaneous output in watts, while generation measures accumulated output in watt-hours. Capacity factor is the fraction of maximum possible generation actually produced over a period ([IEA glossary](https://www.iea.org/glossary), [EIA electricity basics](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/index.php), [EIA on capacity and generation](https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=101&t=3)).

The freshest detailed official world balance I found is for 2022. The United Nations reports 619.2 EJ of total energy supply: 187.5 EJ from oil, 172.3 EJ from coal, 145.7 EJ from gas, 52.4 EJ from biofuels and waste, 29.0 EJ from nuclear, and 32.2 EJ in its electricity and heat category. Fossil fuels supplied 81.6 percent. Final consumption was 426.0 EJ, including 88.4 EJ of electricity. Industry consumed 130.7 EJ, transport 111.8 EJ, and households 85.1 EJ, excluding non-energy uses where applicable ([UN *Energy Statistics Pocketbook 2025*, pages 8-9 and 43-45](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf)).

![Three-stage energy funnel showing 619.2 EJ of total supply in 2022, 426.0 EJ of final consumption in 2022, and 28,600 TWh of electricity in 2025](https://research.gmfoster.com/assets/illustrations/unlimited-energy/01-infographic-energy-funnel.jpg)

The 193 EJ gap between supply and final consumption is not a reserve waiting to be claimed. It includes conversion losses, energy-sector use, and distribution losses. Thermal generation discards heat. Motors, electric vehicles, and heat pumps can often deliver the same service with less final or primary energy than combustion. Accounting conventions also assign different primary-energy values to noncombustible electricity ([EIA on conversion losses](https://www.eia.gov/Todayinenergy/detail.php?id=44436), [EIA on renewable accounting](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=41013), [IEA electrification](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/electricity/electrification)).

This is why I would define the mission as 10× clean electricity, not 10× primary energy. Ten times the useful services could require much less than ten times the primary input if transport, buildings, and industry electrify. Conversely, clean aviation fuel, steel, fertilizer, and round-the-clock power could require far more generation than today's electricity system. A useful target specifies the service, location, reliability, carbon intensity, and delivered price. Exajoules alone are not a product.

For the scale calculations below, I use about 658 EJ as the 2025 total-energy baseline and 28,600 TWh as 2025 electricity consumption. The IEA says demand reached nearly 650 EJ in 2024 and grew by another 8 EJ in 2025. One year is 8,760 hours, and one TWh is 0.0036 EJ ([IEA 2025 global trends](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2025/global-trends), [IEA 2026 global trends](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/global-trends), [IEA *Electricity Mid-Year Update 2026*](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-mid-year-update-2026/executive-summary)).

| Scenario | Annual energy | Average power | Ratio to 2025 electricity |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 total energy | about 658 EJ | 20.9 TW | 6.4 times |
| 2025 electricity consumption | 28,600 TWh, or 103 EJ | 3.3 TW | 1 time |
| 10× electricity | 286,000 TWh, or 1,030 EJ | 32.6 TW | 10 times |
| 100× electricity | 2,860,000 TWh, or 10,296 EJ | 326 TW | 100 times |

Ten times electricity is already 286,000 TWh per year. A literal 10× of all energy is equivalent to 1.83 million TWh, almost 64 times today's electricity. The two ambitions differ by more than a factor of six before anyone chooses a technology. At 100×, the difference is the same: 2.86 million TWh of electricity versus 65,800 EJ of total energy, equivalent to 18.3 million TWh.

## The build is the mission

The world is not starting from zero. In 2025 it added a record 800 GW of renewable capacity, 75 percent of it solar, and almost 110 GW of battery storage. Solar generation grew by 600 TWh, the largest one-year increase ever recorded for any energy source outside post-crisis rebounds. Renewables supplied 34 percent of global electricity, with wind and solar together at 17 percent ([IEA 2026 key findings](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings), [IEA 2026 electricity supply](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/electricity-supply), [IEA battery storage in 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-battery-storage)). Terawatt-scale construction is now an operating industry.

It is still small beside 10×. IRENA reports that utility-scale solar commissioned in 2024 cost a global weighted average of $691 per kW, generated at $43 per MWh, and had a 17.4 percent capacity factor. If solar alone supplied the incremental electricity for the 10× case, with no efficiency gains, I calculate that the world would need about 169 TW of new nameplate capacity. Generation equipment and installation alone would cost about $117 trillion. Spread across 25 years, that is $4.7 trillion per year before storage, transmission, distribution, finance, replacements, or customer equipment. The capacity equals 281 batches of the solar capacity installed in 2025. Manufacturing can grow, so this is a scale ratio rather than a 281-year forecast ([IRENA *Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2024*](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024), [IEA 2026 additions](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/key-findings)).

The solar-only 100×-electricity yardstick is 1.86 PW of new capacity and $1.28 quadrillion of generation investment. Spread across 25 years, $51 trillion per year compares with projected 2025 global energy investment of $3.3 trillion, of which $2.2 trillion goes to clean technologies and $1.1 trillion to fossil fuels ([IEA *World Energy Investment 2025*](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

Land exposes the same difference in scale. An older NREL study found an average total land-use requirement of 3.6 acres per annual GWh for US utility-scale PV. Applying that US value mechanically gives about 3.75 million square kilometers for the 10× increment and 41.25 million for 100×. Rooftops, better modules, agrivoltaics, offshore wind, nuclear, and transmission from better resources would all change the result. The calculation is a stress test, not a proposal to cover that area with one technology ([NREL, *Land-Use Requirements for Solar Power Plants in the United States*](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy13osti/56290.pdf)).

![Solar-only yardstick comparing capacity, capex, land, and build-rate requirements for 10× and 100× electricity](https://research.gmfoster.com/assets/illustrations/unlimited-energy/03-comparison-solar-yardstick.jpg)

No single source carries the mission. Solar, wind, and batteries are commercial, modular, and scaling fastest. The IEA expects renewable generation to rise from 9,900 TWh in 2024 to 16,200 TWh in 2030 under its main forecast, a major expansion that still reaches only about half of today's total generation ([IEA *Renewables 2025*](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2025/renewable-electricity)). Firm sources fill a different role. Fission had 377.08 GW of net operable capacity at the end of 2025, with construction beginning on 12.2 GW during the year. Supplying the 10× increment with nuclear alone, even at a hypothetical 90 percent capacity factor, would require about 32.6 TW, roughly 87 current fleets. The IEA's stated-policy case reaches nearly 650 GW by 2050 ([IAEA PRIS world statistics](https://pris.iaea.org/PRIS/WorldStatistics/WorldTrendNuclearPowerCapacity.aspx), [IEA nuclear in 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-nuclear), [IEA nuclear investment outlook](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-path-to-a-new-era-for-nuclear-energy/outlook-for-nuclear-investment)). Next-generation geothermal could add another firm component. It now meets less than 1 percent of global demand; with major improvements, the IEA estimates as much as 800 GW and 6,000 TWh per year by 2050, about one fifth of today's electricity consumption ([IEA, *The Future of Geothermal Energy*](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-future-of-geothermal-energy/executive-summary)). These sources can strengthen a portfolio without carrying it alone.

The mission does not need fusion or orbital solar to begin. Fusion has crossed a scientific threshold, not a power-plant threshold. An April 2025 National Ignition Facility target produced 8.6 MJ of fusion energy from 2.08 MJ of laser light, while the facility's power-conditioning system stores about 400 MJ from the grid for a shot. LLNL says an inertial-fusion plant would need roughly one million times NIF's shot rate and much better wall-plug efficiency. ITER's revised baseline schedules deuterium-tritium operation for 2039 and targets 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of plasma heating for 400 to 600 seconds. Neither machine is designed to sell net electricity ([LLNL NIF record](https://lasers.llnl.gov/news/target-breakthrough-enabled-fusion-record-nif), [LLNL power conditioning](https://lasers.llnl.gov/about/how-nif-works/power-conditioning-system), [LLNL on higher power](https://str.llnl.gov/str-march-2026/pursuit-higher-power), [ITER baseline](https://www.iter.org/node/20687/new-baseline-prioritize-robust-start-exploitation)). NASA's 2024 space-solar study was similarly sobering. Its two 2 GW systems required 2,321 and 3,960 launches, cost $276 billion and $434 billion, and produced estimated costs of $0.61 and $1.59 per kWh, 12 to 80 times NASA's projected 2050 terrestrial alternatives. Space solar may eventually fit remote or strategic niches, but the study still found major gaps in autonomous assembly, maintenance, and wireless power beaming ([NASA report page](https://www.nasa.gov/organizations/otps/space-based-solar-power-report/), [NASA technical report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230018600/downloads/OTPS%20SBSP%20Report%20Final_Tagged_Approved_1_5_24.pdf)).

## Where abundance gets stuck

Generation costs are becoming less decisive. IRENA found that 91 percent of utility-scale renewable capacity commissioned in 2024 produced electricity more cheaply, on a levelized basis, than the cheapest new fossil alternative. Its averages were $34 per MWh for onshore wind and $43 for utility solar. Those numbers describe lifetime production at the plant. They do not price a balanced megawatt-hour delivered through a constrained grid when a customer wants it ([IRENA 2024 costs](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024)).

The grid is already falling behind. The IEA estimates that the world must add or refurbish more than 80 million kilometers of grids by 2040, roughly the size of the existing system. At least 3,000 GW of renewable projects were waiting in connection queues in 2023. Generation attracted nearly $1 trillion of investment in 2025, versus about $400 billion for grids, and the IEA says grid spending must approach parity early in the 2030s ([IEA grids](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA investment](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)).

Timing makes the imbalance worse. A renewable project can take one to five years, while new grid infrastructure often takes five to fifteen. Cable procurement can take two to three years, large transformers up to four, and some high-voltage direct-current cables more than five. About 1,650 GW of advanced wind and solar projects were awaiting grid connections in 2024, with permitting the largest source of transmission delay in many jurisdictions ([IEA, *Building the Future Transmission Grid*](https://www.iea.org/reports/building-the-future-transmission-grid/executive-summary)).

The United States offers a closer view of the queue. Proposed generation and storage totaled 2,061 GW at the end of 2025. Among projects entering US queues from 2000 through 2020, only 13 percent of requested capacity reached operation and 75 percent was withdrawn. Median time from request to commercial operation now exceeds five years. A queue is not a construction pipeline, but it is strong evidence that network studies and permission can stop equipment developers already want to build ([Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, *Queued Up 2026*](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/backlog-power-plants-seeking-transmission-grid-connection-eased-somewhat-2025-amidst)).

Storage solves hours more readily than seasons. The IEA describes today's batteries as best suited to one-to-eight-hour flexibility. Most systems added in 2025 had about two hours of duration, although four-hour systems were growing. In a transition scenario consistent with tripling renewables, global storage capacity reaches 1,500 GW by 2030, including 1,200 GW of batteries. The Department of Energy's target of $0.05 per kWh for storage lasting more than ten hours is a target for innovation, not a current market price ([IEA batteries](https://www.iea.org/reports/batteries-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary), [IEA battery storage in 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-battery-storage), [DOE Long Duration Storage Shot](https://www.energy.gov/oe/articles/new-report-showcases-how-innovation-can-fast-track-affordable-energy-storage)).

![Delivery chain from generation through interconnection, transmission, firming, and end-use machines to firm energy service](https://research.gmfoster.com/assets/illustrations/unlimited-energy/04-flowchart-bottleneck-chain.jpg)

Materials and manufacturing can expand, but they cannot do so instantly. The leading refining country across key minerals, excluding rare earths, held an average 72 percent share in 2025. The IEA's project pipeline implies a possible 25 percent copper supply deficit by 2035, while investment in critical-mineral mining fell 9 percent in 2025. Prices can induce substitution, recycling, and new mines. They cannot erase lead times ([IEA *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026*](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026/executive-summary)).

The machines consuming the power must change too. Electricity was only 88.4 EJ of 426.0 EJ of final consumption in the UN's 2022 balance, while oil alone was 168.5 EJ. An abundance program therefore includes vehicles, heat pumps, furnaces, electrolyzers, fuel synthesis, and redesigned industrial processes. Commercial heat pumps could technically serve about 20 percent of industrial heat demand, mainly below 120 C; higher-temperature uses remain at an earlier stage ([UN *Energy Statistics Pocketbook 2025*](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf), [IEA *Heat Pump Monitor 2026*](https://www.iea.org/reports/heat-pump-monitor-2026/key-findings)).

Finance and permission can slow construction as decisively as an equipment shortage. Africa has about 20 percent of the world's population but receives only 2 percent of clean-energy investment. Across the continent, overall debt-servicing costs were equivalent to more than 85 percent of total energy investment in 2025, a macroeconomic comparison that shows how little room many governments have to mobilize capital. Falling module prices do not repair a utility balance sheet, remove currency risk, or lower the cost of capital ([IEA *World Energy Investment 2025*](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-investment-2025/executive-summary)). At 10× scale, transmission corridors, mines, reservoirs, reactors, turbines, and solar fields also concentrate local costs. Speed requires credible safety institutions, compensation, community participation, and consent where rights apply.

Cost, volume, location, timing, and finance multiply. For many future loads, the meaningful metric will be firm power at the customer's busbar, including the grid and conversion equipment, rather than the levelized cost at a generator.

## Is cheaper more important than more?

Cheap and plentiful reinforce each other, but they solve different problems. Lower prices make more projects worth attempting and support investment in new demand. Greater volume lets more projects operate at once. Location and timing determine whether either matters to a particular customer.

William Stanley Jevons saw part of this in 1865. He argued that improvements in the efficiency of coal use could raise total coal consumption by making coal-powered activity cheaper and more widespread. What became known as the Jevons paradox began as an argument about industrial organization, not a universal numerical law ([Jevons, *The Coal Question*](https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html?chapter_num=2)).

Modern rebound estimates vary by technology, market boundary, and time horizon. Gillingham and colleagues found positive direct rebound effects in many energy services, while emphasizing the difficulty of economy-wide estimates and the weak basis for assuming complete "backfire" everywhere. A service can use less energy per unit while lower prices cause people to buy more units ([Gillingham and colleagues, 2014](https://www.rff.org/documents/1036/RFF-DP-14-39.pdf)).

That is not an argument against abundance. If the additional supply is clean, more cooling, mobility, computation, water, and industrial output can be the point. It does mean that cheap energy should not be assumed to reduce emissions or material throughput by itself. Emissions and local harms still need prices or limits, and common resources still need protection.

There is also no global abundance if the average hides exclusion. In 2023, an estimated 666 million people lacked basic electricity access and 2.1 billion lacked clean cooking. About 85 percent of those without electricity lived in sub-Saharan Africa. The UN's 2022 balance reports 22.1 GJ of energy supply per person in Africa and 272.3 GJ in Northern America, a gap above twelve to one ([WHO, *Tracking SDG 7: The Energy Progress Report 2025*](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/2025-tracking-sdg7-report), [WHO release](https://www.who.int/news/item/25-06-2025-energy-access-has-improved--yet-international-financial-support-still-needed-to-boost-progress-and-address-disparities), [UN *Energy Statistics Pocketbook 2025*, page 45](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/energystats/pubs/documents/2025pb-web.pdf)).

A 10× system clustered in a few industrial regions can coexist with scarcity elsewhere. Distribution needs last-mile networks, solvent utilities, affordable appliances, clean-cooking equipment, trustworthy institutions, and finance in local currency. In some places a small reliable system creates more welfare than another gigawatt connected to a saturated market. I would track two outcomes: total firm clean supply and a floor of affordable energy services for every person.

## At 100×, the planet enters the calculation

A literal 100× increase in total energy use is physically possible in the narrow sense that available solar energy is much larger. It is not simply a bigger version of the same infrastructure program.

Using NASA's mean Earth radius of 6,371 km gives a surface area of about 5.10 × 10^14 square meters. If all 658 EJ of today's annual energy use appeared as additional heat spread evenly over the planet, the average would be 0.041 W/m2. At 10× it would be 0.41 W/m2; at 100×, 4.09 W/m2. The IPCC's assessed effective radiative forcing from doubled carbon dioxide is 3.93 W/m2. The climate response would differ because location, altitude, timing, and feedbacks differ, but the comparison shows why waste heat eventually stops being negligible ([NASA Earth radius reference](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160012759/downloads/20160012759.pdf?attachment=true), [IPCC AR6 Working Group I, Chapter 7](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-7/)).

This is an upper-bound warning, not a forecast. Solar panels mostly intercept radiation already entering Earth's climate system, so their net effect depends on changed albedo and where the energy would otherwise have been absorbed or reflected. Fossil, nuclear, and geothermal heat are more directly additive. Earth absorbs about 240 W/m2 from sunlight on average, almost sixty times the literal 100× flow. The resource is there; at that scale, the source and disposal of heat become design choices ([NASA on Earth's energy budget](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/)).

Land points in the same direction. The crude solar-only 100×-electricity yardstick occupied 41.25 million square kilometers using an older US land-use factor. A real portfolio would look different, but any literal 100× program would force decisions about where energy is collected, which landscapes and communities carry the infrastructure, and whether some production belongs offshore or eventually off-planet.

## A mission we can build

Current deployment shows that multi-terawatt replication is real. Under existing policies, the IEA expects 4,600 GW of renewable additions from 2025 through 2030. The remaining mission is to turn record years into sustained construction while expanding transmission, long-duration firming, and high-temperature industrial electrification at the same pace ([IEA *Renewables 2025*](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-2025/renewable-electricity)). It does not require waiting for fusion or space solar.

I would set the milestone as **cheap, firm, clean energy service**, measured at the place and time it is needed. That target rewards generation only when it reaches a useful machine, and it keeps distribution inside the mission rather than treating access as an afterthought.

Ten times as much clean electricity would give Mars programs cheaper propellant, longevity labs more experimental capacity, cities more water, and industry cleaner routes to fuels and heat. Each mission would keep its own harder bottlenecks. Abundant energy is a multiplier rather than a substitute for invention. It is hard enough to occupy generations and concrete enough to begin now.

## Claims I could not verify

I could not verify a complete, public, line-by-line 2025 global energy balance by source using the same accounting basis as the UN table. I therefore use the UN's 2022 detailed mix and the IEA's 2025 aggregate update, with the years labeled rather than spliced.

I could not verify a defensible integrated energy budget or settlement cost for a self-sustaining city on Mars. Published mission concepts choose different crews, cargo, propulsion, life support, and local-production assumptions.

I could not verify a universal numerical estimate for economy-wide Jevons backfire. The original claim is a historical argument, and modern empirical estimates depend on scope and time horizon.

I could not verify a current commercial fusion-electricity cost because no fusion plant sells electricity. Target gain, wall-plug energy, repetition rate, plant availability, and net electric output are different measures.

I could not verify that space-based solar, fusion, or any present technology will make electricity "too cheap to meter." The primary roadmaps describe targets and capability gaps, not guaranteed delivered prices.

I could not verify an ethically neutral welfare value for 10× or 100× energy. The consequences depend on source, use, location, ownership, and distribution.
