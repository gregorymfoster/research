## The question, and why a specialized agent is a candidate answer

The prior post argued, following Hamming, that a problem is important only if you have an attack on it. Consequence alone is not importance; a great problem with no line of attack is just a great frustration. It also argued that problems ripen when tools arrive, and that the 2026 move is to re-rank old problems against what AI can now do.

This post takes those two principles and puts them under load. A specialized agent, meaning a purpose-built domain system like AlphaFold in structural biology or AlphaProof in mathematics rather than a general chatbot, is exactly the kind of new tool that principle two is about. The tempting story: the tool has arrived, so the problems ripen, so the short-on-attacks bucket empties out. That story is mostly wrong, and the interesting part is why. The evidence from the specialized agents that already exist is sharp about where they win and where they stall, and the dividing line is not the importance of the problem. It is a property of the problem's feedback structure. Where a cheap, trustworthy way to check an answer exists, these systems have already produced real results. Where it does not, they produce demos and contested claims.

The cases below are traced to their primary sources, then scored against the twelve problems from the prior survey.

## The clean win: weather forecasting

Start with the case that has the least ambiguity. Medium-range weather forecasting is a domain where a specialized model beat the physics-based incumbents outright, on the incumbents' own metrics, and the incumbents agreed.

DeepMind's GraphCast, published in *Science* in 2023, is a graph neural network trained on four decades of ECMWF reanalysis data. Its own paper reports that it produced more accurate 10-day forecasts than the ECMWF's high-resolution deterministic system, HRES, on [90% of 1,380 verification targets, and produced a full 10-day forecast in under a minute](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adi2336). That is an empirical result reported by the authors and published in a peer-reviewed journal. The follow-up, GenCast, published in *Nature* in 2024, is a diffusion model that produces probabilistic ensembles rather than a single trajectory, and its paper reports that it [beat the ECMWF's ENS ensemble, the top operational system in the world, on 97.2% of 1,320 targets, generating a 15-day ensemble forecast in about eight minutes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08252-9).

Why is this the clean case? Because weather forecasting hands you the answer key for free. Every forecast is checked against what actually happened a few days later, at every grid point, for decades of history. The training signal is dense and continuously renewed by the atmosphere itself. You do not need a human to grade a forecast, and you do not need to wait years to find out if it was good. The verifier is reality, and it reports back on a schedule of hours to days. Note also that the win is a prediction, not an intervention. GraphCast does not change the weather; it forecasts a system that will reveal the truth regardless. That distinction matters for everything below.

## Structural biology: prediction is solved, therapy is not

Protein structure is the obvious next case, the one the 2024 Nobel put on every list, and here the record needs to be split carefully into what was predicted, what was designed, and what was shipped to a patient.

AlphaFold's 2021 *Nature* paper is the foundational empirical result: it reports predicting protein structures with [a median backbone accuracy of 0.96 Angstroms at CASP14, against 2.8 Angstroms for the next best method](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03819-2), which is roughly the width of an atom and competitive with experimental methods. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized this work. The result is verified, and it has the same structure as the weather case: CASP is a blind competition scored against experimentally determined structures, so the field had a cheap, trusted verifier before the agent arrived. Protein structure prediction was a problem with an answer key.

Design is a harder and more recent frontier, and the results are real but earlier-stage. The Baker lab's RFdiffusion, published in *Nature* in 2023, is a diffusion model for generating protein backbones, and its paper reports experimentally characterizing [hundreds of designed proteins including de novo binders, with a designed binder's structure confirmed by cryo-EM to be nearly identical to the design model](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06415-8). A 2025 follow-up in *Nature* extends the method to [de novo antibody design validated by yeast display and cryo-EM](https://www.ipd.uw.edu/publication-pdfs/339/b141e332aeaefab1bd76ee450896a4bf/s41586-025-09721-5.pdf). Chai Discovery's Chai-2, released in 2025 as a company technical report and bioRxiv preprint, reports a [16% experimental hit rate for fully de novo antibody design across 52 targets, with at least one binder found for 50% of targets from 20 or fewer designs, and a 68% hit rate for miniproteins](https://www.chaidiscovery.com/news/introducing-chai-2). That last set is a first-person institutional claim in a non-peer-reviewed preprint, and it should be read as such; Engin Yapici, an independent reviewer who went through the [technical report](https://chaiassets.com/chai-2/paper/technical_report.pdf), found the reporting unusually transparent but noted the usual caveats about framework selection and structure-selection choices, and Chai's co-author responded to those points in [the public discussion](https://medium.com/@enginyapici/i-tried-to-poke-holes-in-chai-2s-antibody-design-paper-here-s-what-i-found-7e51f5581c7d). The summary that survives the caveats: design now works well enough in silico that the number of wet-lab candidates you must screen has dropped by orders of magnitude. That is the win, and it is large.

![A left-to-right flowchart. On the left, a tight three-box loop of design, predict, and score labeled fast computational loop, feedback in seconds. A long handoff arrow leads to a large heavy box labeled clinical trial with a closed gate, feedback in years. The caption reads: the agent compresses the fast part, then waits for the slow verifier.](https://research.gmfoster.com/assets/illustrations/specialized-agents/03-flowchart-fast-then-slow.jpg)

What has not yet happened is the last mile. Isomorphic Labs, the DeepMind spinout built on AlphaFold, is the natural place to look for shipped therapeutics, and as of early 2026 its own statements put the first drugs still short of the clinic. CEO Demis Hassabis said at Davos in January 2026 that the company [now expects its first clinical trials by the end of 2026](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/google-backed-isomorphic-labs-delays-clinical-trial-timeline-5871841), a one-year slip from the previous target, and The Pharma Letter's tracking describes its oncology and immunology programs as [in IND-enabling studies with nothing yet dosed in humans](https://www.thepharmaletter.com/ones-to-watch/isomorphic-labs). This is the pattern that matters for medicine: the agent radically accelerates the design-and-predict step, which has fast computational feedback, and then the process rejoins the slow biological verifier of trials, where feedback is measured in years and no model shortcuts it. Structure prediction is solved. Curing a disease is not, and the reason is that the human body remains the only trustworthy verifier of a drug, and it is slow.

## Antibiotic discovery: a real molecule, from a cheap assay

Machine-learning-guided antibiotic discovery is a cleaner medical case than de novo therapeutics, and the reason is instructive. In 2020, a team at MIT published in *Cell* that a deep neural network trained on the antibacterial activity of about 2,335 molecules, then run over chemical libraries, identified [halicin, a structurally unusual compound with broad-spectrum activity that cleared infections in mouse models](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0092867420301021). In 2023 the same lineage of work published in *Nature Chemical Biology* the discovery of [abaucin, a narrow-spectrum antibiotic against the priority pathogen *Acinetobacter baumannii*, from a model trained on roughly 7,500 molecules](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41589-023-01349-8). Both are empirical, peer-reviewed results.

The feedback structure repeats here. Whether a molecule inhibits bacterial growth in a dish is a cheap, fast assay. You can generate training labels by the thousand and check a prediction in a day. The agent is doing triage over an enormous chemical space, and the space has a fast oracle attached. This is the same shape as weather and structure prediction, which is why it worked. To be precise about what was delivered: these are validated leads with mouse-model efficacy, not approved drugs. The fast assay verifies the near end of the pipeline; the far end still runs through the slow clinical verifier.

## Mathematics: a formal verifier changes everything

Mathematics is the domain where the feedback-structure argument is most vivid, because mathematics has a machine-checkable notion of truth. A proof written in a proof assistant like Lean either compiles or it does not, and if it compiles it is correct with respect to its stated theorem. That is an oracle as hard as any in weather or protein structure.

DeepMind's AlphaProof, whose methodology was published in *Nature* in November 2025, is built directly on this. It is an AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning system that trains on [millions of auto-formalized problems and learns to find proofs in Lean, using the compiler as its reward signal](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09833-y). At the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad, AlphaProof together with AlphaGeometry 2 [solved four of six problems for 28 of 42 points, the level of a silver medalist, one point below gold](https://deepmind.google/blog/ai-solves-imo-problems-at-silver-medal-level/), with the solutions graded by the mathematicians Timothy Gowers and Joseph Myers. The result required multi-day computation rather than the human time limits, which the paper states plainly. This is an empirical, verified result, and its engine is the formal verifier.

A year later the bar moved to gold, with a nuance worth flagging. At IMO 2025, an advanced version of Gemini Deep Think [officially earned 35 of 42 points, a gold-medal score certified by the IMO coordinators](https://deepmind.google/blog/advanced-version-of-gemini-with-deep-think-officially-achieves-gold-medal-standard-at-the-international-mathematical-olympiad/), and an experimental OpenAI reasoning model [reported the same score under contest conditions, self-graded by three former medalists](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/google-openais-ai-models-win-milestone-gold-global-math-competition-2025-07-21/). The nuance is that the 2025 gold came from general-purpose reasoning models writing natural-language proofs, not the specialized formal system, so the gold result is partly outside this post's scope. The DeepMind and OpenAI results also differ in evidentiary weight: one was certified by the competition, the other self-reported.

The most interesting mathematical results are the ones that are actually new. DeepMind's AlphaEvolve, described in a 2025 report, is an evolutionary coding agent that uses an evaluator as its fitness function, and its report claims genuine novelty: a procedure to [multiply two 4x4 complex-valued matrices with 48 scalar multiplications, the first improvement in that setting over Strassen's 1969 algorithm in 56 years](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2506.13131), plus new constructions on over 50 open mathematical problems, matching the best known on about 75% and surpassing it on about 20%, including an improvement on an Erdős minimum-overlap problem and a kissing-number construction in 11 dimensions. The matrix result is real but should be read with the caveat the Maplesoft mathematician John May raised: it holds over the complex numbers, it trades multiplications for [many more additions, and it does not touch the asymptotic complexity records that other methods already hold](https://www.mapleprimes.com/maplesoftblog/230205-About-That-4x4-Matrix-Multiplication-Result). It is a provably correct new object, which is what matters here, verified by construction.

The Erdős-problems saga of late 2025 and early 2026 is the cautionary counterpart, and it teaches the same lesson from the other side. In October 2025, OpenAI staff publicly claimed GPT-5 had solved ten previously open Erdős problems; the mathematician Thomas Bloom, who maintains the problem database, [clarified that "open" on his site meant only that he personally did not know a solution, and that the model had surfaced existing published solutions rather than creating new ones](https://the-decoder.com/leading-openai-researcher-announced-a-gpt-5-math-breakthrough-that-never-happened/). The claims were walked back. Then, in January 2026, came a genuinely different event: Erdős problem #728 was, per a formal writeup, [resolved autonomously by a combination of GPT-5.2 Pro and Harmonic's Aristotle prover, with the proof written and checked in Lean and Terence Tao vouching for its autonomous status after a literature search found no prior solution](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07421v3). The difference between the embarrassment and the real result is exactly the verifier: the first was an unchecked natural-language claim about novelty, the second was a machine-checked proof.

Tao's own first-person testimony is the most valuable witness here, and he is careful. In his essay [*Mathematics in the age of AI*](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16753), based on a 2026 ICM lecture, he argues that autoformalization has begun to shift mathematics "from proof scarcity to proof abundance" and that the culture should move emphasis from being first to a proof toward proof digestion: exposition, refereeing, canonicalization. In a [living summary of his views](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/ai-views.html) he stresses two boundaries: a proof assistant certifies the formal statement, not that it matches your intent, so human review is reduced but not eliminated; and Lean "formalizes a proof a human already has" rather than discovering proofs on its own. On the autonomous cases he is deflationary about difficulty, estimating that today's tools resolve only [on the order of one to two percent of the problems tried, and that a problem sitting open for fifty years often means nobody seriously tried, not that it resisted all effort](https://the-decoder.com/terence-tao-says-gpt-5-2-pro-cracked-an-erdos-problem-but-warns-the-win-says-more-about-speed-than-difficulty/). That calibration is right: the progress is real and driven by verifiers, and it is much smaller than the October claims made it sound.

## Materials science: the cautionary tale about missing verifiers

Materials discovery is the domain that most cleanly shows what happens when the announcement outruns the verifier, and it repays a closer look because it shows the failure mode the clean wins avoid.

DeepMind's GNoME work claimed an order-of-magnitude expansion in known stable materials, with millions of predicted structures and hundreds of thousands flagged as stable. Two materials chemists, Anthony Cheetham and Ram Seshadri, published a perspective in *Chemistry of Materials* examining the claims and concluded that they found [scant evidence for compounds meeting the trifecta of being credible, novel, and useful, that many predictions were trivial adaptations of known compounds, and that these were better described as crystalline inorganic compounds than as "materials," a term that implies demonstrated utility](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/full/10.1021/acs.chemmater.4c00643). Their objection is precise: a prediction of thermodynamic stability is not the same as a synthesized, characterized, useful material, and the gap between the two is where the actual science lives.

The A-Lab, published in *Nature* in 2023, tried to close that gap with an autonomous synthesis robot, and reported that over 17 days it [synthesized 36 of 57 target compounds, a 63% success rate](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w). Solid-state chemists disputed the central claim almost immediately. A detailed critique concluded that the [automated X-ray diffraction analysis was flawed, that the Rietveld refinements were poor, and that in their assessment none of the produced compounds were genuinely new, with many being misidentified known phases](https://storage.prod.researchhub.com/uploads/papers/2024/01/09/challenges-in-high-throughput-inorganic-material-prediction-and-autonomous-synthesis.pdf). Robert Palgrave, one of the critics, told *Chemistry World* that [the AI could not perform the characterization task well enough to correctly identify the compounds](https://www.chemistryworld.com/news/new-analysis-raises-doubts-over-autonomous-labs-materials-discoveries/4018791.article). The paper was corrected in early 2026, and *C&EN* reported that the correction [conceded the materials were "not necessarily new to science," while critics maintained that the deeper problem, the model's failure to account for real-world disorder, went unaddressed](https://cen.acs.org/research-integrity/Nature-robot-chemist-paper-corrected/104/web/2026/01).

None of this means materials AI is fraudulent. The verifier here is genuinely hard: confirming that you synthesized a specific novel crystal requires expert crystallography, and the automated interpretation was not up to it, so the loop closed on a proxy, and truth never entered it. When the verifier is weak or is itself an unreliable model, the agent optimizes the proxy and the field gets a database of things that are credible on paper and absent in the world. Materials science is the domain where the checking step is the bottleneck, and until that step is cheap and trustworthy, the wins will be contested.

## Fusion, chips, and the rest: control problems win, contested claims linger

A few more cases fill in the pattern.

Plasma control for fusion is a quiet, real win of the same type as weather. DeepMind and the Swiss Plasma Center published in *Nature* in 2022 a reinforcement learning controller that [autonomously shaped and maintained tokamak plasmas on the TCV reactor, including advanced snowflake and simultaneous droplet configurations](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04301-9). This worked because control problems come with a simulator: the policy is trained against a physics model of the tokamak and then transferred to hardware, with the reactor's sensors providing the feedback. It is a demonstration of control, and the paper claims nothing about net energy. But it is a genuine experimental result, and its structure is the familiar one, a fast simulator plus objective real-world feedback.

Chip design is the contested case that mirrors materials. DeepMind's chip-placement method, published in *Nature* in 2021 and later named AlphaChip, claimed superhuman macro placements, and the company reports the method is [deployed across generations of its TPUs](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08032-5). Academic groups disputed the performance claims: Igor Markov's meta-analysis in *Communications of the ACM* argued that the [method lagged simulated annealing, commercial tools, and human designers, and that key methodology was missing from the paper](https://cacm.acm.org/research/reevaluating-googles-reinforcement-learning-for-ic-macro-placement/), while DeepMind responded that the [critics failed to pre-train, used insufficient compute, and did not train to convergence](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.10053). The dispute is unresolved in the public literature, which is itself the point: chip placement has a proxy objective (wirelength and congestion estimates) that is not the same as the final routed, fabricated chip, and when the proxy and the truth diverge, so do the claims.

## Science automation: creative engine or literature search?

The most ambitious specialized agents try to automate discovery itself, and the results here are the most oversold and the most instructive to read closely.

Google's AI co-scientist produced the case Google itself leads with in a collaboration with Imperial College London on antimicrobial resistance. The published account in *Cell* reports that the system's [top-ranked hypothesis about how cf-PICI genetic elements spread across bacteria, by hijacking the tails of unrelated phages, matched the mechanism the Imperial team had already established experimentally](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.08.018). This is a first-person institutional claim from the collaborating teams, and it is narrower than it may sound: the team had already reached and confirmed the answer through years of unpublished lab work, and the test was whether the AI could reach the same hypothesis from the literature. It recapitulated a known-to-them conclusion quickly; it did not independently run the experiments that made the conclusion true. That is valuable as hypothesis generation, and it is not the same as autonomous discovery.

Sakana AI's AI Scientist is the most literal attempt at end-to-end automation, and its own reporting is unusually candid about the ceiling. Published in *Nature* in 2026, the system generated three papers submitted, with organizer consent, to an ICLR workshop; [one received an average reviewer score of 6.33, above the acceptance threshold, and was withdrawn per a pre-agreed protocol](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10265-5). The accepted paper reported a negative result, and Sakana's own internal review concluded that [none of the three met the bar for a main conference](https://sakana.ai/ai-scientist-first-publication/). So the honest reading is that a fully AI-generated paper cleared a workshop bar once, which is a real milestone, and that the same team judged the work below the standard of a serious venue. The verifier here, peer review, is soft and noisy, and the result sits right at the edge of what a soft verifier will pass.

## Education: the case that needs a different kind of agent

Education is where the specialized-agent argument is most interesting, because the wins so far come from chatbots with good scaffolding, and the gap between that and a true agent points at what an agent would add.

The strongest evidence is a randomized trial. Harvard physicists ran a within-subject RCT with 194 students and reported in *Scientific Reports* that a custom AI tutor produced [more learning in less time than an active-learning classroom, with students also reporting more engagement and motivation](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6). The World Bank's Nigeria trial is the largest field result: a six-week after-school program using GPT-4 with teacher facilitation and reasoning-oriented prompts produced [a 0.3 standard-deviation gain overall and about 0.23 in English, which the authors estimate as equivalent to 1.5 to 2 years of ordinary schooling, among the most cost-effective interventions in its class](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099548105192529324). Both are empirical, and both come with real caveats: the Harvard study covered short lessons at middle-order cognitive levels, and the Nigeria result depended on structured routines, teacher facilitation, and guardrails against over-reliance, not a chatbot left alone with students.

Neither of these is a specialized agent in the AlphaFold sense. They are general chatbots wrapped in careful prompts and human structure. A specialized tutoring agent would add what a chatbot lacks: a persistent model of the individual student, memory of what they have and have not mastered, an explicit curriculum state, and a mastery model that decides what to teach next. Education's feedback structure is favorable in one respect (you can measure learning gains with tests) and unfavorable in another (the measurement is delayed, noisy, and confounded by everything else in a student's life). The tests above are what makes the domain attackable at all. The open question is whether a stateful agent beats a well-prompted chatbot, and there is not yet a clean RCT that isolates that.

## The pattern: what the wins share

Set the cases side by side and they sort on a single variable: the feedback structure.

The wins all have a cheap, trustworthy verifier that returns a signal fast. Weather is checked by the atmosphere within days. Protein structure is checked by CASP against experiment. Antibiotic candidates are checked by a growth assay in a day. A Lean proof is checked by a compiler in seconds. A fusion controller is checked by a simulator and then by sensors at kilohertz. A matrix-multiplication algorithm is checked by construction. Where that verifier exists, the agent can search an enormous space and be trusted, because every candidate is filtered through something that cannot be fooled by a plausible-looking answer.

The stalls all have a weak, slow, or missing verifier. A drug's true test is a multi-year trial, and no model shortcuts it, which is why structure prediction is solved and Isomorphic's first dose is still ahead. A new material's test is expert synthesis and characterization, and when that step was handed to an unreliable automated analysis, the field got contested claims. A chip's true test is fabrication, and the placement proxy diverged from it. A research paper's test is peer review, which is soft, and the agent's output landed right at its edge. Autonomous discovery's test is running the confirming experiment, and the co-scientist recapitulated an answer the humans had already confirmed; it confirmed nothing itself.

The candidate hypotheses from the framing question mostly collapse into this one. A simulator or oracle is just a cheap verifier you can query at will. A narrow, well-posed search space matters because it makes the verifier's job tractable. Data density matters because it is how you get a verifier cheaply, in the form of labels. The unifying property is not any of these alone; it is that the problem lets you check an answer far more cheaply than you can produce one. That is precisely the "problems with feedback" principle from the prior survey, sharpened: the feedback has to be cheap and fast, not merely to exist in principle. A defined endpoint measured in years does not ripen under a specialized agent. A defined endpoint measured in seconds does.

One more distinction runs through the wins: prediction and design in silico are where agents thrive, and physical or institutional intervention is where they rejoin human timescales. The agent compresses the fast, checkable part of the pipeline and then hands off to the slow verifier. That is why the medical wins are all upstream of the clinic and the education wins are all measured with tests.

## Scoring the twelve big problems

With that pattern, here is how the prior survey's twelve problems score for a specialized-agent attack. Importance is no longer the question; each problem gets asked whether it hands an agent a cheap, fast, trustworthy verifier to optimize against.

The strongest scores go to problems with a built-in oracle. Scientific progress itself scores highest, because a large part of it decomposes into subproblems that do have verifiers: structure prediction, molecule triage, formal proof, forecasting, control. This is consistent with the prior post's claim that scientific progress is the meta-option that multiplies the others, and it is why so many of the real wins above are, at bottom, science-acceleration tools. Pandemic preparedness, taken as defensive public-health engineering, scores well on the parts that inherit the biology verifiers: vaccine-antigen and binder design have the fast in-silico-plus-assay loop that antibody design has demonstrated, and diagnostics and surveillance are prediction problems with measurable ground truth. The slow clinical verifier still gates deployment, exactly as with any therapeutic. Energy scores well on its physical-control and forecasting layers: grid operation, plasma control, and renewable-output forecasting are simulator-and-sensor problems of the kind that already work, even though building new supply remains capital-and-permitting-bound rather than agent-bound.

The middle scores go to problems where an agent helps a human-run loop rather than closing its own. Education is attackable because learning gains are measurable, and the RCTs prove a real effect, but the verifier is slow and noisy and the current wins are chatbots-plus-teachers, so a specialized agent is promising rather than proven. Global health was already in the money-constrained-with-attack bucket in the prior survey; an agent adds diagnostic and logistics triage with measurable endpoints, but the binding constraint there is delivery and funding, which no agent removes. Aging biology sits here too, and the prior survey's diagnosis translates directly into this post's language: agents can accelerate the upstream target-discovery and design work, which inherits the fast biology verifiers above, but the field's definitive verifier is healthy human lifespan, which reports back in decades. The prior post said validated endpoints are the bottleneck; in verifier terms, the highest-leverage work in aging is building a cheaper verifier that regulators trust, biomarkers and aging clocks, not pointing a bigger model at the search.

The weak scores go mostly to the problems the prior survey put in the short-on-attacks bucket, joined by the split layers of two problems it ranked higher, and this is where the sequel's core claim faces its real test. The claim to test was that a specialized agent could move a problem from short-on-attacks into attackable. For most of these it cannot, and the reason is uniform: they lack a cheap verifier. Loneliness has no objective, fast signal of the thing that matters; you can measure a proxy like self-reported connection, but optimizing an engagement proxy is how you get something that makes loneliness worse. Fertility decline has feedback loops measured in decades and is bound up in economics, culture, and institutions that no model checks. Democratic backsliding and state capacity are institution-bound, adversarial, and lack any endpoint an agent could optimize without a human deciding what "better" means, and the adversarial part means any proxy will be gamed. Nuclear risk is high-consequence and low-frequency, which is the worst possible shape for a learning system: there is essentially no dense feedback, and the tail events you care about are the ones you cannot afford to sample. Housing splits: its cost-of-building layer has an engineering verifier and could yield to design-and-simulate agents, while its politics-of-permitting layer is institution-bound and does not. AI safety and alignment is the interesting mixed case: the measurement layer, evals and interpretability, is genuinely attackable because you can define and check metrics, which is why the prior survey called it a talent-constrained problem with an attack in hand, but "is this system aligned" as a whole has no trustworthy verifier yet, which is the entire difficulty.

So the sequel's core move survives, but weakly and selectively. A specialized agent moves a problem into the attackable column only when it also supplies or inherits a cheap verifier. Where it cannot, the problem stays short on attacks, and asserting otherwise produces the demos and contested claims the opening warned about.

## What a builder-funder can actually do

For the individual with capital, skill, and time that the prior post addressed, the pattern turns into concrete advice.

First, hunt for the verifier before the problem. Pointing an agent at an important problem is the obvious move and usually the wrong one; the higher-leverage move is finding an important problem that already has a cheap, fast, trustworthy way to check answers, or building that verifier where it is missing. The A-Lab's failure was a verifier failure, and the most useful thing a materials-focused builder could do is make automated crystallographic characterization actually trustworthy, because that unlocks every synthesis agent downstream. In many domains the checking infrastructure is both the bottleneck and the durable asset.

Second, build the institution around the loop, per the prior survey's principle that new tools need new institutions. The wins above are models plus the thing that grades them. CASP is an institution. The Lean and Mathlib ecosystem is an institution, and Tao's [integrated explicit analytic number theory network](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/01/15/the-integrated-explicit-analytic-number-theory-network/) is a builder explicitly constructing the formalization infrastructure that makes AI-assisted proof trustworthy at scale. A funder who endows the verifier and the benchmark often does more good than one who funds another model, because the verifier is a public good the whole field optimizes against.

Third, target the fast, checkable part of a slow pipeline and be honest about the handoff. In medicine that means funding design and triage, where agents already work, while accepting that the clinical verifier still sets the calendar. In education it means building the stateful tutoring agent the chatbots gesture at, and running the RCT that isolates whether student memory and a mastery model beat a good prompt, because that RCT is the verifier for the whole thesis and it does not yet exist.

Fourth, distrust the demo, per the hits-based principle that you should back things that look contrarian but right. The materials and chip cases show that a splashy claim in a top journal can be an optimized proxy masquerading as a truth. A builder's edge is to demand the verifier's provenance for every claimed win: what checked this, how cheaply, and could it be fooled. Where the answer is "a human will eventually check, in years" or "another model checked," discount heavily.

Personal fit still multiplies everything. The verifier-rich domains reward a builder who can hold both the model and the domain's ground truth in their head, because the value is created at the seam between them.

## Claims I could not fully verify, and how they are labeled

A handful of things in the record deserve a flag rather than a footnote.

Chai-2's antibody-design numbers are a first-person institutional claim in a non-peer-reviewed preprint and company report, and I labeled them as such in the text. The only independent scrutiny I found was a blog analysis, so the hit rates should be treated as the company's own reporting until a formal replication exists.

AlphaChip's production deployment ("taped out in TPUs across Google datacenters") is a first-person institutional claim by DeepMind that I could not independently verify, and it is contested precisely on the point of whether deployment proves superiority over prior methods. I flagged the dispute as unresolved in the public literature.

The OpenAI IMO 2025 gold score is self-reported and self-graded by three former medalists, in contrast to the DeepMind score, which was certified by the IMO. I distinguished the two in the text; only the DeepMind result is externally certified.

The AI co-scientist result is genuine and peer-reviewed, but I was careful to label what it is: the AI recapitulated a hypothesis the Imperial team had already confirmed experimentally but not published, so it counts as verified hypothesis generation rather than independent autonomous discovery. Any stronger framing overstates it.

Tao's one-to-two-percent success-rate figure for AI on Erdős problems is his personal estimate stated in an interview, and he himself notes that failures go unreported so the true rate is hard to pin down. I labeled it as his estimate.

Finally, I deliberately did not fetch anything on weapons or misuse capability, and I scored pandemic preparedness purely as defensive public-health engineering from the inlined summary and the biology sources above, per the scope constraint. Nothing in this post rests on risk literature I did not read.
